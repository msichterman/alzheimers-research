## Abstract

### Abstract

#### INTRODUCTION

Discovery and development of new therapies for Alzheimer's disease (AD) are urgently needed to address the world's growing population of individuals on the AD pathophysiological continuum. Clinicaltrials.gov is a resource for studying drugs in development for treatment of AD.

#### RESULTS

There are currently 158 drugs in 192 AD trials. Of the agents in trials, 39% are small molecule disease targeting therapies (DTTs); 34% are biologic DTTs; 18% are cognition enhancing symptom targeted therapies (STTs); and 10% are STTs being developed to treat neuropsychiatric symptoms of AD. Currently active trials require 54,728 participants of which 38,417 are in Phase 3. The biopharmaceutical industry sponsors 59% of AD trials including 72% of Phase 3 trials. Repurposed drugs represent 35% of the drugs in trials.

#### DISCUSSION

The AD drug development pipeline has a growing number of trials and drugs in trials. A diverse array of AD pathophysiological processes is being addressed by drugs in trials.

### Highlights

There are 158 drugs for the treatment of Alzheimer's disease in the 2026 drug development pipeline. Repurposed agents account for 35% of Alzheimer's disease drugs in development. 55,000 participants are required to populate all currently active trials. 73% of drugs in the pipeline are disease targeted therapies whose goal is to slow disease progression. The biopharmaceutical industry sponsors 59% of all trials and 72% of Phase 3 trials.

## BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) Is the most common cause of cognitive impairment in later life and is projected to increase steadily with the aging of the population. Considering the continuum of AD from asymptomatic biomarker positive, to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) with evidence of AD pathology, to AD dementia, the number of affected individuals in the United States will increase from 46.7 million (biomarker positive, no symptoms), 2.43 million (MCI), and 3.65 million (AD dementia) in 2017 to 75.68 million (biomarker positive without symptoms), 5.7 million (MCI), and 9.3 million (AD dementia) in 2060. Although calculations, vary, studies suggest that, by 2060, approximately 90 million persons in the United States will have AD‐type changes in the brain with or without manifest symptoms of cognitive impairment.

There has been important progress in developing new disease targeted therapies (DTTs) for AD in the form of anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies (MABs) and success in development of symptom targeted therapies (STT) for the treatment of agitation associated with dementia due to AD., There are no DTTs approved for preclinical AD or for moderate to severe AD dementia; no new classes of cognition enhancing agents have been approved since 2004; and there are no treatments for non‐agitation neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) such of AD as psychosis, depression, or apathy. There is a great unmet need for DTT and STT drug development to address the needs of the growing population of individuals with AD.

We describe AD drug development as reflected in the clinical trials for AD related therapies registered on clinicaltrials.gov . We describe agents in Phase 1, 2, and 3 clinical trials for preclinical AD (biomarker changes without symptoms), MCI due to AD, and AD dementia. We note biomarkers included in the trial as reported on the federal registry. We sum the number of participants needed for all currently active trials. We discuss the global distribution of trial activity and describe trial sponsorship. Our goal is to provide information useful for trial planning, conduct, and analysis and to assist stakeholders in understanding the landscape of evolving AD therapeutics.

### METHODS

Our methodologic approach builds on our previous AD pipeline studies., The data reviewed for this report were collected from clinicaltrials.gov , a clinical trial registry maintained by the US National Library of Medicine of the National Institutes of Health (NIH). All clinical trials that have at least one site in the US, are conducted under a Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Investigational New Drug (IND) authorization, or involve a drug that is manufactured in the United States or its territories must be registered on clinicaltrials.gov within 21 days of enrolling the first participant.

For this report, data in clinicaltrials.gov are accessed in the application programming interface (API) of the registry and transferred to the database of the UNLV Clinical Trial Observatory and the Cleveland Clinic Laboratory of Network Medicine for analysis and interpretation.Raw data are retrieved from clinicaltrials.gov as they are posted, and the data (in Json format) are parsed according to the API's data structure to extract more than 30 key data fields. Automated filtering is performed combining rule‐based programming and manual curation by experts to identify and characterize trials testing AD pharmacological interventions. All extracted and annotated data are stored in a relational database using PostgreSQL for querying and analysis.

The Index Date for this review is January 1, 2026. Numbers, percentages, and other numerical data provided reflect clinical trials active on the Index Date. Trials reaching their completion date prior to the Index Date or initiated and registered after the Index Date are not included. We capture data on all active trials identified including those labeled as recruiting, active but not recruiting, enrolling by invitation (e.g., open‐label extensions of trials with enrollment limited to those participating in a previous trial of the same drug), and not yet recruiting (e.g., registered on clincaltrials.gov but no participants have been enrolled). Trials that are suspended, terminated, completed, withdrawn, or unknown (defined as having no status update within the past 2 years) are described in the report but not included among the active clinical trial summaries and calculations.

#### RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review : Four mechanisms of action have been verified by therapeutic benefit in Alzheimer's disease (AD): cholinergic agents, an N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate agent, three anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies, and a multi‐transmitter antipsychotic for reduction of agitation. Agents that address other target processes and study of combination approaches are needed. The current AD drug development pipeline includes 192 clinical trials assessing 158 candidate therapies. There are 36 drugs in Phase 3, 84 potential treatments in Phase 2, and 45 agents in Phase 1. Disease targeting therapies (DTTs) account for 73% of agents in trial; cognition enhancing symptom targeted therapies (STTs) contribute 18% of agents in the pipeline; and drugs targeting neuropsychiatric symptoms account for 10% of drugs in active trials. Interpretation : Most agents in trials are intended to prevent or delay the onset of AD or slow the progression of disease symptoms. A few agents in trials have the goal of improving memory and cognition or reducing behavioral changes occurring in patients with AD. The biopharmaceutical industry sponsors most trials, especially those of drugs in late‐stage development. Repurposing plays an important role in AD drug development and repurposed agents comprise almost half of Phase 2 drugs. The federal registry clinicaltrials.gov is a rich resource of information on drug development for AD. Future directions : Review of the current drug development pipeline provides the basis for optimism regarding the emergence of new therapies for patients with AD. There are a robust number of trials, and agents in trials target a variety of pathophysiological disease processes. Biomarkers are increasingly used for participant identification and as pharmacodynamic outcomes. Progress is evident in clinical trial design, integration of biomarkers into trials, and emergence of promising candidate therapies.

We include trials in Phase 1, Phase 1/2, Phase 2, Phase 2/3, and Phase 3. When trials span two phases, we use the higher number for our calculations. We do not include Phase 0 or Phase 4 clinical trials in this report. We note novel agents in each phase; if an agent is being assessed in more than one phase, it is counted only once in totals of novel agents.

Our search terms include “Alzheimer's disease” and terms related to AD (e.g., dementia of the Alzheimer type, prodromal AD, preclinical AD, and MCI). We do not include trials whose participants have dementia of any cause or in which AD is included with other dementias. MCI as a manifestation of a non‐AD condition such as MCI of Parkinson's disease is excluded.

We capture comprehensive data regarding trials available on clinicaltrials.gov including the name of the test agent, trial title, national clinical trial (NCT) numerical identifier, actual start date, projected primary end date, whether a biomarker was required for eligibility or served as a primary or secondary outcome, and whether the agent was repurposed (e.g., approved for another indication or repositioned during development). To determine if a drug is repurposed (e.g., approved for a non‐AD indication), we compare the agents in the AD drug development pipeline to the available version of DrugBank ( https://go.drugbank.com/ ).

We describe where the trials were performed (North America; non–North America with no North American sites; global, North American and non–North American sites included in the trial). We identify the “lead sponsors” of trials as industry or not industry. AD trials not funded by industry are funded by the National Institute on Aging (NIA), other federal agencies such as the Veterans Administration, non‐US governmental agencies, advocacy groups, or philanthropy.

We calculated the number of active trials sites in seven global regions. An “active” site was participating in at least one clinical trial on the Index Date.

We note if the trial population is cognitively normal with biomarker evidence of AD, has MCI/prodromal AD, or exhibits mild, moderate, or severe AD dementia. If other trial designations are used such as “early AD” (MCI plus mild AD dementia), we use the population as described in clinicaltrials.gov . Not all trials used biomarkers to determine participant eligibility.

We derive the target process of the agent in the trial based on the descriptive categories of the Common Alzheimer's Disease Research Ontology (CADRO). CADRO categories include amyloid‐beta protein (Aß); tau; apolipoprotein E (APOE), lipids, and lipoprotein receptors; transmitter receptors; neurogenesis; inflammation and immune dysfunction; oxidative stress; cell death; proteostasis/proteinopathies; metabolism and bioenergetics; vascular factors; growth factors and hormones; synaptic plasticity/neuroprotection; gut–brain axis; circadian rhythm; environmental factors; epigenetic regulators; multitarget; unknown target; and “other”. Within each of these categories of target processes, agents can have one or several specific mechanisms of action (MoAs). The MoAs presented in Tables , , are derived from clinicaltrials.gov or from review of the relevant literature and informational websites such as ALZFORUM.

Based on mechanistic and target information, we classify agents as DTTs or as STTs, depending on whether the declared therapeutic purpose is to impact a specific aspect of the pathophysiology of AD with the intention of delaying the occurrence of symptoms or slowing clinical decline or is intended to improve symptoms present at baseline in the trial (cognitive impairment or NPS). This classification can be ambiguous for some therapies; agents may have multiple therapeutic mechanisms; and drugs may have both DTT and STT properties. We identify the principal therapeutic intent of the agent in the trial based on trial characteristics, literature review, and information from the trial sponsor. We divide DTTs into biologics (e.g., MABs, vaccines, antisense oligonucleotides [ASOs], etc.) and small molecules (e.g., drugs usually taken orally and less than 500 Daltons in molecular weight). We divide STTs into those with putative cognition enhancing properties and those intended to ameliorate NPS such as psychosis, agitation, or depression.

In this report, we do not include trials of non‐pharmacologic therapeutic approaches such as exercise, lifestyle interventions, cognitive–behavior therapies, caregiver interventions, supplements, or medical foods. We do not include trials of devices, gene therapies, or stem cell therapies. We do not include biomarker trials if there is no intervention.

### RESULTS

#### Overview

On the Index Date of January 1, 2026, we identified 192 clinical trials for AD assessing 158 drugs. This included 54 trials assessing 36 drugs in Phase 3; 89 trials assessing 84 drugs in Phase 2; and 49 trials assessing 45 drugs in Phase 1 (Figure ). Twelve of the 192 trials in the pipeline are long‐term extensions of trials with treatment by the agent used in a prior trial.

The AD drug development pipeline is comprised primarily of DTTs. There are 116 DTTs representing 73% of drugs in development; DTTs account for 134 (70%) of current clinical trials. Sixty‐two (53%) of DTTs are small molecules and 54 (47%) are biological therapies. Phase 3 is comprised of 21 (58%) DTTs; Phase 2 has 61 (73%) DTT agents; Phase 1 has 39 (87%) DTTs. Twenty‐nine of the drugs in the pipeline (18% of agents in trials) are putative cognition enhancers, and 16 (10% of agents in trials) drugs target NPS.

Seventeen of the CADRO categories of AD pathophysiology are targeted by at least one drug in current clinical trials. Thirty‐eight drugs (24%) in the pipeline target neurotransmitter receptors; 28 agents (18%) address inflammation/immune processes; 25 treatments (16%) target Aß protein‐related pathophysiology; 15 agents (9%) target tau‐related processes; eight drugs (5%) address synaptic plasticity/neuroprotection; seven therapies (4%) target metabolism and bioenergetics; five drugs (3%) target growth factors and hormones; four drugs (3%) are posited to treat oxidative stress, and four (3%) address proteostasis/proteinopathy processes; three agents (2%) target vascular pathology of AD; two agents (1%) each target APOE/lipids, gut–brain axis, circadian rhythm disturbances, and epigenetic factors; one (0.6%) each target neuronal loss and neurogenesis; and nine (6%) drugs are described as multi‐target agents.

Fifty‐nine trials (31% of currently active trials) in the AD drug development pipeline are new since the last Index Date (January 1, 2025). There are 14 (26% of Phase 3 trials) new trials in Phase 3, 27 (30% of Phase 2 trials) new trials in Phase 2, and 18 (37% of Phase 1 trials) new trials in Phase 1. Of trials that were active in the pipeline on the Index Date (January 1, 2025) of the last report, 34 clinical trials were completed, nine were terminated, two were withdrawn, and eight are of unknown status (no activity registered on clinicaltrials.gov in the past 2 years).

Repurposed agents approved for non‐AD indications include 56 drugs (35% of drugs in current trials) and 73 (38%) currently active trials. Among the repurposed drugs, six (11%) are DTT biologics, 32 (57%) are DTT small molecules, 12 (21%) are putative cognition enhancers, and eight (14%) are being assessed for the treatment of NPS (some agents are being assessed for both cognition enhancement and NPS). Repurposed agents account for 11% of DTT biologics, 52% of DTT small molecules, 41% of cognition enhancing agents, and 50% of treatments for NPS in the AD drug development pipeline.

All stages of the AD continuum are represented in current clinical trials. Ten trials (5%) involve cognitively unimpaired persons with biomarker or genetic evidence of AD; 67 trials (35%) include participants with MCI due to AD or prodromal AD (with or without biomarker confirmation of AD pathology); 52 trials (27%) involve participants with early AD (MCI or mild AD dementia); 102 trials (53% of active trials) enroll patients with mild AD dementia; and 63 trials (33%) include participants with moderate or severe AD dementia (with or without biomarker confirmation of AD pathological changes). (Some participant categories overlap accounting for why the sum of all the trials listed exceeds the number of active trials.)

Of all currently active trials for which geographic data are given ( N = 177), 65 (37%) are conducted only in North America; 59 (33%) include only areas outside of North America; and 53 (30%) include clinical trial sites both in North America and outside of North America (e.g., are “global”).

The biopharmaceutical industry sponsors 113 (59%) of all clinical trials in the current AD pipeline, including 39 (72%) Phase 3 trials; 43 (48%) Phase 2 trials; and 31 (63%) Phase 1 trials.

#### Phase 3 trials

There are 54 Phase 3 trials studying 36 drugs (Index Date January 1, 2026; Table ; Figure ). Of the agents in Phase 3, 21 (58%) are DTTs; nine (25%) target cognition enhancement; and six (17%) address NPS (one STT is for an “other” indication, i.e., seizures). Of the DTTs, 10 (47%) are small molecules and 11 (52%) are biologics. There are five prevention trials assessing DTTs in the Phase 3 AD pipeline, five assessing biologic DTTs, and one studying a DTT small molecule (one trial is assessing both a biologic and a small molecule). Seven trials (13% of Phase 3 trials) are long‐term extensions of trials of drugs that have completed a prior trial of the same drug.

#### Phase 2 trials

There are 89 Phase 2 trials in the 2026 AD drug development pipeline, comprising 46% of all currently active trials. Phase 2 involves 84 unique drugs representing 53% of all pharmacologic therapies in active AD trails (Table ; Figure ). Of the agents in Phase 2, 61 (72% of Phase 2 drugs) are DTTs; 17 (20%) target cognition enhancement; and eight (9%) address NPS. Of the DTTs, 23 (38%) are biologics and 38 (62%) are small molecules.

Thirty‐eight (45%) Phase 2 agents are repurposed. Twenty‐eight (74%) repurposed drugs in Phase 2 are DTTs, including one (11% of Phase 2 repurposed agents) biologic DTT and 24 (63% of Phase 2 repurposed agents) small molecule DTTs. Seven (18%) Phase 2 repurposed agents are putative cognition enhancing agents, and four (11%) Phase 2 repurposed agents are being developed to treat NPS.

There are four long‐term extension trials for participants that have completed a prior trial of the same agent (4% of Phase 2 trials).

There are 2 trials of preclinical AD populations in Phase 2; 40 trials for MCI/prodromal AD participants; 27 trials including participants with early AD; 56 trials for mild AD participants; and 33 and eight trials including moderate and severe AD dementia, respectively (categories are not mutually exclusive, allowing the sum to exceed 100%). There are six (7%) trials among the 89 Phase 2 trials in the AD pipeline aiming to forestall (prevent) some aspect of AD.

Among the 89 active Phase 2 trials, 27 (30%) are new to the pipeline since January 1, 2025.Compared to the last Index Date (January 1, 2025), 17 Phase 2 trials were completed, three trials were terminated, two trials were withdrawn, and three trials are of unknown status (no recorded activity for 2 years).

Sixteen CADRO categories of AD pathophysiology are targeted by at least one drug in current Phase 2 clinical trials (Figure ). Nineteen drugs (23% of Phase 2 agents) in the AD pipeline target neurotransmitter receptors and 19 (23%) target inflammation/immune dysfunction. Eight drugs (9%) each target Aß or tau biology; five agents (6%) address synaptic plasticity; three drugs (3%) each, target vascular factors or growth factors and hormones; two agents (2%) target oxidative stress and two target circadian rhythm disturbances; and one drug (1%) targets each of the APOE/lipids, neurogenesis, proteostasis/proteinopathy, metabolism and bioenergetics, gut–brain axis, and epigenetic regulator processes. Seven (8%) Phase 2 drugs are reported to be multi‐modal targeting agents, and two (2%) had undisclosed mechanisms.

The biopharmaceutical industry sponsors 43 (48%) of Phase 2 trials. Biopharmaceutical companies sponsor six (16%) repurposed agents in Phase 2 trials and 35 (76%) novel, non‐repurposed agents in Phase 2.

Review of the global distribution of Phase 2 trials with location information reveals that 39 (48%) are conducted only in North America; 24 (30%) are conducted only outside North America; and 18 (22%) are conducted at both North American and non–North American regions.

Phase 1 of the 2026 AD drug development pipeline includes 49 trials assessing 45 drugs (Index Date January 1, 2026; Table ). Of the agents in Phase 1, 39 (87%) are DTTs; three (7%) target cognition enhancement; and three (7%) address NPS. Of the DTTs, 24 (62%) are biologics and 15 (38%) are small molecules.

There are six (13%) repurposed agents in Phase 1 trials. Five of the six repurposed agents are DTTs, including one biologic and four small molecules. One Phase 1 repurposed agent is being developed to treat NPS. There is one long‐term extension trial of an agent that had been assessed in a completed trial of the same agent.

There are 22 (45%) Phase 1 trials testing AD‐relevant drugs and comprised of healthy volunteers with no clinical or biomarker evidence of AD. Four (8%) Phase 1 trials include participants with preclinical AD; 19 (39%) Phase 1 trials involve MCI participants; 10 (20%) trials include participants with early AD; 14 (29%) trials involve mild AD dementia; and five (10%) include participants with moderate AD dementia. There are no Phase 1 trials specifically for participants with severe AD; one trial includes participants with AD dementia of unspecified severity (categories are not mutually exclusive).

There are 18 new trials in Phase 1 comprising 37% of the trials in this phase of the AD pipeline. Compared to the last Index Date (January 1, 2025), 15 Phase 1 trials were completed, no Phase 1 trials were terminated or withdrawn, and four trials are of unknown status (no recorded activity for 2 years).

Processes involved in 13 CADRO categories of AD pathophysiology are targeted by at least one drug in current Phase 1 clinical trials (Figure ). The CADRO categories of amyloid, tau, and inflammation/immune dysfunction each has eight (18% of the Phase 1 drugs for each of these targets) related agents in Phase 1 trials. Six (13%) drugs in Phase 1 target neurotransmitter receptors, and three (7%) address metabolic/bioenergetic targets. Two agents (4%) are being developed for each of the categories of oxidative stress and synaptic plasticity. One (2%) agent is in development for each of the CADRO targets involved of APOE/lipids, cell death, proteostasis/proteinopathy, growth factors and hormones, gut–brain axis, and epigenetic regulators. There were two Phase 1 agents (4%) with undisclosed mechanisms.

The biopharmaceutical industry sponsors 31 (63%) Phase 1 trials. Biopharmaceutical companies sponsor one of six Phase 1 trials for repurposed agents and 30 (70%) trials of novel, non‐repurposed agents in Phase 1.

Review of the global distribution of Phase 1 trials reveals that 20 (43%) are conducted only in North America; 23 (49%) are conducted only outside North America; and four (8%) are conducted in both North American and non–North American regions. Phase 1 trials collectively require 2,350 participants for the currently registered trials.

#### Longitudinal trial observations

The annual review of the AD drug development pipeline began in 2016 allowing longitudinal review of pipeline activity. We assessed the number of trials and the number of agents in the pipeline over the decade of 2017–2026. The number of trials increased by approximately 35%, and the number of agents in trials increased by approximately 40% during this interval (Figure ). The number of trials active in the pipeline is the sum of new trials that have entered since the previous Index Date and previously registered trials that have not yet completed. Examination of the three most active areas of DTT drug development revealed that the percentage of the pipeline devoted to inflammation/immune dysfunction drugs has increased from 6% to approximately 20%, tau targeted agents have increased from 6% to approximately 20%, and amyloid targeted agents have decreased from 33% of the pipeline to approximately 20% of agents in the pipeline (Figure ). The percentage of inflammation/immune dysfunction drugs and of amyloid targeted agents has been stable since 2022.

#### Biomarkers in trials

Most active trials include a biomarker in the trial design — 47 Phase 3 trials (87%), 79 Phase 2 trials (89%), and 33 Phase 1 trials (67%) — include a biomarker for at least one context of use. Among the 192 clinical trials, 96 (50%) have an inclusion biomarker for trial eligibility, with amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) identified as the most common biomarker modality ( N = 49). Fluid biomarkers are used for inclusion in 58 (60% if trials in which an inclusion biomarker was incorporated) trials, 44 trials measure biomarkers in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analytes, 26 trials measure blood‐based biomarkers, and one trial has saliva as a biomarker source.

Fifty‐two (27%) active AD trials have a biomarker as a primary outcome measure (nine in Phase 3, 32 in Phase 2, and 11 in Phase 1) with or without a clinical measure as a concomitant primary outcome. Imaging was used as a primary outcome in 29 trials (56% if trials in which a biomarker functioned as a primary outcome) (one trial had ocular imaging as a primary outcome). There are 43 trials (46% of trials with biomarker as a primary outcome) using a fluid biomarker as a primary outcome measure; with 16 measuring a blood‐based biomarker, 10 measuring CSF analytes, one measuring exosomes from an undefined fluid, and one measuring changes in stool samples. Aβ biomarkers are used as a primary outcome measure in 16 trials (31% of trials with a primary outcome biomarker; five in Phase 3, nine in Phase 2, and two in Phase 1), and tau biomarkers are used in 14 trials (27% of trials with a biomarker as a primary outcome; two in Phase 3, nine in Phase 2, three in Phase 1). The most common outcome biomarkers are Aβ PET (11), MRI (eight), and tau PET (four).

#### Trial participants

To fully populate currently active trials, 54,728 participants are required: 38,417 in Phase 3; 13,961 in Phase 2; and 2,350 in Phase 1. Of the total number of required participants, 70% are needed for Phase 3 trials, 26% are required for Phase 2 trials, and 4% for Phase 1 trials.

Trial participants are divided among trials for DTTs (39,390; 72% of participants); trials for cognition enhancing agents (6,658; 12%); trials for dugs addressing NPS (8,560 participants; 16%); and trial for “other” symptoms (120; 0.2%).

#### Global trial distribution

Phase 3 trials require the largest number of patients and correspondingly the largest number of sites and participating regions. Sixty‐three percent of Phase 3 trials include both North American and non–North American sites; 22% of Phase 2 trials include both North American and non–North American sites; and 9% Phase 1 trials are conducted in North American and non–North American sites. Forty‐seven of 53 (89%) global trials are sponsored by the biopharmaceutical industry.

Five percent of DTT small molecule trials are conducted globally; 50% of DTT biological agent trials are conducted globally; 23% of trials for cognition enhancing agents are global; and 42% of trials for NPS therapies are conducted globally.

We calculated the global distribution of unique clinical trial sites active on the Index Date (January 1, 2026) and found that there are 3156 sites in North America and 3607 sites in non–North American regions including 1363 in Western Europe/Israel, 661 in Eastern Europe/Russia, 571 in Asia (not including Japan), 516 in Japan, 321 in South America/Mexico, and 175 in South Africa, Australia, or New Zealand.

#### Repurposed agents

The 56 repurposed agents and 73 trials of repurposed drugs in the 2026 AD drug development pipeline represent 35% of the total number of drugs in trials and 38% of all trials. Repurposed agents account for 42% of drugs in Phase 3, 45% of drugs in Phase 2, and 13% of drugs in Phase 1. Of repurposed agents in trials, 57% are DTT small molecules, 11% are DTT biological agents, 21% are STTs addressing cognition enhancement, and 14% are STTs for NPS of AD. The biopharmaceutical industry sponsors most of the repurposed agents in Phase 3 (47%; N = 7) and relatively fewer of the repurposed drugs in Phase 2 (16%; N = 7) and Phase 1 (6%; N = 1).

#### Trial funders

The biopharmaceutical industry sponsors 59% of all clinical trials in the 2026 AD drug development pipeline, including 72% of Phase 3 trials, 48% of Phase 2 trials, and 63% of Phase 1 trials. Biopharmaceutical industry‐sponsored trials account for 42% of DTT small molecule trials, 68% of DTT trials of biological agents, 57% of trials of cognition enhancers, and 78% of STTs trials addressing NPS. Non‐industry funders include federal agencies (e.g., NIH in the United States, non‐US governmental agencies), philanthropic and advocacy organizations, academic medical centers, and public‐private collaborations (if industry is not identified as the lead sponsor).

#### Combination therapies

There are few trials involving combination therapies in the 2026 pipeline. Phase 3 combination trials include MABs targeting tau and Aß ( NCT01760005 ; NCT05269394 ), a gamma secretase modulator plus an anti‐Aß plaque MAB ( NCT06996730 ), a combination of a dopamine agonist (rotigotine) and a cholinesterase inhibitor (rivastigmine) ( NCT06702124 ), and a combination of a muscarinic cholinergic agonist (xanomeline) and a peripheral cholinergic antagonist (trospium) (KarXT 10 trials). Phase 2 combination trials include a tau vaccine and an anti‐Aß MAB ( NCT07167966 ), MABs targeting tau and Aß ( NCT06602258 ), two agents targeting hypertension to determine the impact on tau and Aß ( NCT05331144 ), and a combination of a repurposed antibiotic (ciprofloxacin) and repurposed anti‐inflammatory agent (celecoxib)( NCT06185543 ). The combination of two reverse transcriptase inhibitors (emtricitabine plus tenofovir alafenamide) is being assessed in a Phase 1 clinical trial involving participants with MCI ( NCT07210528 ). Most trial descriptions on clinicatrials.gov do not specify if patients on MABs are eligible for participation; those allowing participants on MABs may be viewed as assessing combination therapies.

#### Drugs scheduled to complete Phase 3 and Phase 2 trials in 2026

Eight Phase 3 trials will reach their primary completion date in 2026 including trials of AR1001, a phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE 5) inhibitor ( NCT05531526 ); caffeine, an adenosine receptor blocker ( NCT04570085 ); donepezil, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor ( NCT04661280 ); metformin, an insulin sensitizer ( NCT04098666 ); valiltramiprosate, an amyloid anti‐aggregation agent ( NCT06304883 ); Wujia Yizhi, a Chinese herbal therapy ( NCT06534723 ); and a muscarinic cholinergic agonist, xanomeline plus trospium for the treatment of AD psychosis ( NCT05511363 ; NCT06585787 ). The Phase 3 trial of donanemab in preclinical AD ( NCT05026866 ) could read out in 2026 if enough events occur to trigger the analysis in the time‐to‐event design. In addition, there are 11 drugs in 14 Phase 3 trials scheduled for primary completion prior to 2026 that have not been reported as completed, terminated, or withdrawn. These trials could have end‐of‐study reports in 2026.

Twenty‐nine Phase 2 clinical trials will be completed in 2026 providing information across a variety of Aß, tau, hormonal, senolytic, inflammatory, transmitter, epigenetic, and vascular disease processes. In addition, there are 19 drugs in 21 Phase 2 trials whose primary completion date was reported to be prior to 2026 and have not yet been updated on clinicaltrials.gov as completed, terminated, or withdrawn. These delayed trial reports could provide information in 2026.

### DISCUSSION

The 192 clinical trials and 158 novel agents in the 2026 AD drug development pipeline expands on the 182 clinical trials assessing 138 drugs present in the 2025 pipeline. The 59 new trials registered since the Index Date of January 1, 2025, is only slightly larger than the 56 new trials initiated in the previous year. The larger number of active trials observed in 2026 may reflect longer treatment and recruitment periods with fewer trial completions in the past year.

Biomarkers have transformed clinical trials with the ability to confirm the diagnosis of AD for trial eligibility, stratify or enroll patients based on likelihood of progression, monitor therapeutic effects longitudinally, and verify the pharmacodynamic effect of the candidate therapy as a study outcome. Fifty percent of current trials use a biomarker for eligibility. Many currently active trials were registered before diagnostic biomarkers were widely available, and it is anticipated that the proportion of trials using diagnostic biomarkers will increase. Twenty‐seven percent of trials have a biomarker as a primary outcome measure (with or without a clinical measure as a concomitant primary outcome). These trials anticipate a change in the biomarker to verify the mechanism of action, demonstrating substantial optimism for the biomarker's pharmacodynamic performance.

Compared to the 2025 pipeline, the 2026 pipeline has more transmitter‐based treatment candidates (plus eight), multi‐target agents (plus six), inflammatory/immune function modulating drugs (plus four), epigenetic drugs (plus two), and more agents addressing oxidation, cell death, proteostasis/proteinopathies, and circadian rhythm disturbances (plus one each). In 2026, there are fewer drugs addressing in the pipeline for neurogenesis, metabolism and bioenergetics, and synaptic plasticity (one fewer in each category). Between 2025 and 2026, there have been no changes in the number of drugs addressing targets related to Aß, tau, APOE/lipids, vasculature, growth factors and hormones, or the gut–brain axis.

Seventeen CADRO categories are represented among the targeted disease processes addressed by drugs in the 2026 pipeline. For each CADRO target process, numerous drug MoAs are included. For example, the inflammation/immune dysfunction category includes 10 agents targeting primarily the microglia, nine targeting cytokines, six addressing both microglia and cytokines, and three directed at some other inflammation/immune related function. In addition, several agents in the multi‐target category are posited to have important effects on inflammation and immune function. Together, the total number of mechanisms represented among drugs in the pipeline is very large and is anticipated to be informative regarding key disease processes and the consequences of modulating them.

There are 56 repurposed drugs in the 2026 pipeline (35% of pipeline drugs) compared to 46 repurposed agents (33% of the pipeline) in 2025. Our analyses demonstrate that involvement of industry in repurposing differs substantially from its involvement in development of novel compounds. In Phase 2, industry is responsible for advancing six repurposed agents and 35 novel agents; this contrasts with non‐industry sponsors that are advancing 11 novel agents and 32 repurposed agents. In Phase 3, industry is advancing 15 novel agents and eight repurposed agents; non‐industry sponsors are advancing nine novel agents and eight repurposed agents. Repurposing represents 16% of the industry pipeline and 57% of the non‐industry pipeline. Our observations suggest that industry sponsors typically assess few repurposed compounds in Phase 3, possibly reflecting the lack of intellectual property for many of the generic agents and the difficulty of generating a return on investment required for development of these agents. The pattern emphasizes the importance of repurposing in Phase 2 as an opportunity for non‐industry sponsors such as academic medical centers to advance research workforce training, drug development tool creation, and trial infrastructure development. These activities translate to benefits for drug development by all sponsors across the drug development ecosystem.

This report has important limitations. All data are derived from clinicaltrials.gov . This registry is comprehensive but not exhaustive, and some drugs in clinical trials — particularly those conducted in non–North American sites — may not be registered and are not included in this report. Registration of US trials on clinicaltrials.gov is mandated but not completely standardized and irregularities of data entry could affect our report. We assign categories of DTT or STT based on clinicaltrials.gov , sponsor information, and published reports. These categories may not accurately characterize the intended purpose of all compounds. Our database team adheres to good practices in data management and governance, but errors in data capture or interpretation are possible.,

This review of currently active AD clinical trials and the drugs being assessed indicates that AD drug development is a robust enterprise with an increased number of trials and drugs in trials in 2026 compared to 2025 and an approximately 40% increase in drugs and trials since 2017. A diverse array of pathophysiological processes is addressed by drugs in the pipeline. Biomarkers are increasingly used for trial eligibility as well as being integrated as pharmacodynamic outcomes. Phase 2 proof‐of‐concept studies are the innovation engines of AD drug development and are populated by the largest number of trials in the pipeline. Non‐industry sponsors account for a substantial number of Phase 2 trials (52%). There have been no new drug approvals for AD since 2023, and the overall failure rate of AD drug development is high. Better target identification, improved ascertainment of potentially responsive patient populations, enhanced use of biomarkers to inform understanding of drug effects, and more stringent criteria for advancing drugs to late‐stage development are anticipated to improve the success rate of AD drug development.
