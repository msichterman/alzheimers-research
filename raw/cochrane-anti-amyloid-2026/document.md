# Amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies for people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease

## Abstract

### Abstract

#### Rationale

Alzheimer’s disease is a neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia. Aggregated amyloid‐beta protein deposits are implicated in its pathogenesis. Amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies (sometimes represented as Aβ‐mAbs) are potentially disease‐modifying for Alzheimer’s disease: through the clearance of amyloid in the brain, they may slow cognitive and functional decline.

#### Objectives

To assess the clinical benefits and harms of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies aducanumab, bapineuzumab, crenezumab, donanemab, gantenerumab, lecanemab, ponezumab, remternetug, and solanezumab in people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease.

#### Search methods

We searched CENTRAL, MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, and two clinical trials registries (Clinicaltrials.gov and WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform), and we undertook reference checking and citation research. The most recent search date was 7 August 2025.

#### Eligibility criteria

We included randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that lasted at least 12 months and compared amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies with placebo or no treatment in people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease. We included both parallel‐group and cluster designs.

#### Outcomes

Our outcomes of critical importance were: cognitive function; dementia severity; functional ability; any amyloid‐related imaging abnormality (ARIA), which includes oedema (E) and haemorrhage (H); any symptomatic ARIA E and H; symptomatic brain haemorrhage; serious adverse events; and any‐cause mortality. We analysed data at 12, 18, 24, and over 24 months of treatment.

#### Risk of bias

We used the Cochrane risk of bias tool RoB 2 to assess the risk of bias in outcomes of critical importance.

#### Synthesis methods

We meta‐analysed results for each outcome within each comparison using the inverse variance method and the random‐effects model. We used GRADE to assess the certainty of evidence for each outcome as very low, low, moderate, or high.

#### Included studies

Overall, we included 17 studies with 20,342 participants. The mean age of participants in the studies ranged from 70 to 74 years. Seven studies enroled only participants with mild dementia, and one study enroled only participants with mild cognitive impairment. The remaining studies included a mixed population. The mean duration of participants' cognitive impairment ranged from 17 to 52 months.

The 17 studies assessed seven different amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies: aducanumab (n = 3), bapineuzumab (n = 4), crenezumab (n = 2), donanemab (n = 1), gantenerumab (n = 4), lecanemab (n = 1), and solanezumab (n = 2). All used placebo as a comparison. Eleven studies lasted 18 months, four lasted 24 months, and two lasted more than 24 months.

All studies were funded by the pharmaceutical industry.

#### Synthesis of results

Below, we report the results of the studies at 18 months.

Cognitive function

Compared to placebo, amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies probably result in little to no difference in cognitive function as measured by the ADAS‐Cog (Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive) scale (standardised mean difference (SMD) −0.11, 95% confidence interval (CI) −0.16 to −0.06; 13 studies, 9895 participants; moderate certainty).

Dementia severity

Amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies may result in little to no difference in dementia severity as measured by the CDR‐SB (Clincal Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes) scale (SMD −0.12, 95% CI −0.24 to −0.00; 9 studies, 8053 participants; low certainty).

Functional ability

Amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies probably result in little to no difference in functional ability as measured on the ADCS‐ADL (Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study ‐ Activities of Daily Living) scale (SMD 0.09, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.16; 3 studies, 3478 participants; moderate certainty) and may result in a small increase in functional ability if measured with the ADCS‐iADL (Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study‐Instrumental Activities of Daily Living) scale (SMD 0.21, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.32; 1 study, 1252 participants; low certainty) or ADCS‐ADL‐MCI (Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study ‐ Activities of Daily Living for Mild Cognitive Impairment) scale (SMD 0.23, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.33; 4 studies, 2802 participants; low certainty).

Adverse events

Amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies probably result in a small increase in the occurrence of any ARIA E (ARD (absolute risk difference) 107 more per 1000, 95% CI 77 more to 148 more; 11 studies, 13,595 participants; moderate certainty) and probably little to no difference in symptomatic ARIA E (ARD 29 more per 1000, 95% CI 22 more to 38 more; 2 studies, 3522 participants; moderate certainty) or symptomatic ARIA H (ARD 4 more per 1000, 95% CI 1 fewer to 31 more; 1 study, 1795 participants; moderate certainty).

Three studies assessing any ARIA H showed heterogeneous results (I 2 = 81%), which prevented pooled analysis.

At 18 months, amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies do not increase serious adverse events (ARD 6 more events per 1000, 95% CI 10 fewer to 26 more; 9 studies, 11,904 participants; high certainty) or overall mortality (ARD 2 more events per 1000, 95% CI 3 fewer to 11 more; 7 studies, 9733 participants; high certainty).

We judged the overall risk of bias as low for the outcomes of serious adverse events and mortality. We had some concerns about the overall risk of bias for efficacy outcomes, mainly due to the risk of functional unblinding (i.e. participants and investigators correctly guessing whether a participant is receiving the active drug or placebo because of noticeable side effects).

#### Authors' conclusions

The effect of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies on cognitive function and dementia severity at 18 months in people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease is trivial, while on functional ability, it is small at best. Amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies increase the risk of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities. Both desirable outcomes and adverse events were inconsistently reported in the studies included in the review.

Successful removal of amyloid from the brain does not seem to be associated with clinically meaningful effects in people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease. Future research on disease‐modifying treatments for Alzheimer’s disease should focus on other mechanisms of action.

### Plain language summary

Are medicines (anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies) that reduce the build‐up of abnormal proteins in the brain an effective treatment for people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease, and do they cause unwanted effects?

Key messages

- In people with mild memory and thinking problems (mild cognitive impairment (MCI)) or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's disease, laboratory‐produced medicines (anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies) that target and remove potentially damaging build‐ups of amyloid proteins in the brain, probably result in little to no difference in the decline in memory functioning and thinking ability, or in how severe dementia symptoms are, compared with placebo (sham treatment) 18 months after the start of treatment.
- Anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies probably cause more brain swelling and tiny (micro) bleeds than placebo. They do not increase other serious unwanted effects or deaths compared with placebo.
- Successful removal of amyloid proteins from the brain does not seem to be associated with clinically meaningful improvements in people with MCI or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease. Future research on disease‐modifying treatments for Alzheimer’s disease should focus on other treatments.

What is Alzheimer’s disease?

In Alzheimer’s disease, brain cells die following the build‐up of proteins (called amyloid plaques). Alzheimer’s disease affects people’s memory and thinking abilities. Symptoms are usually mild to begin with and do not interfere with everyday life. This is called ‘mild cognitive impairment’ (MCI). Over time, it can progress to mild dementia, where memory and thinking difficulties are serious enough to interfere with everyday activities. About 15% of people with MCI will develop dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease within two years. It is the most common form of dementia among older people.

What are anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies?

Antibodies are made by the body as a defence against disease. They can also be produced in a laboratory for use as a medical treatment. Anti‐amyloid antibodies are designed to target the amyloid proteins that cause plaques due to Alzheimer’s disease, and remove them from the brain. They are ‘monoclonal’ because they only target amyloid proteins. Removing amyloid proteins from the brain may slow the progression of Alzheimer’s disease.

What did we want to find out?

We wanted to know if anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies are an effective medicine for people with MCI or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease. We evaluated whether they slowed down:

- the decline in memory and thinking;
- the decline in ability to manage everyday activities; and
- the worsening of dementia symptoms.

We also wanted to know if they caused any unwanted effects.

What did we do?

We searched for studies that investigated one or more anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies to treat people with MCI or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease, compared with placebo (sham treatment that does not contain any medicine but looks identical to the medicine being tested and is delivered in the same way).

We summarised the results of the studies, and rated our confidence in the evidence, considering aspects such as study sizes and methods.

What did we find?

We found 17 studies that were carried out in different countries and involved 20,342 people. The average age across studies was from 70 to 74 years. All studies were funded by companies that produced the anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies.

Main results

After 18 months of treatment, anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies:

- may make little to no difference to how bad people’s dementia symptoms are (9 studies, 8053 people);
- probably make little to no difference in the decline in memory and thinking ability (13 studies, 9895 people) or the ability to manage everyday activities (3 studies, 3478 people);
- may result in a small improvement in more complex everyday tasks, such as shopping, managing finances, taking medication, and using transportation (1 study, 1252 people);
- probably result in a small increase in the occurrence of brain swelling. For every 1000 people using monoclonal antibodies, 119 developed brain swelling compared with only 12 of 1000 people using placebo (11 studies, 13,595 people);
- may result in a small increase in microbleeds in the brain (3 studies, 4308 people);
- do not increase other serious unwanted effects as defined by the study authors (9 studies, 11,904 people); and
- do not increase deaths from any cause (7 studies, 9733 people).

What are the limitations of the evidence?

Our confidence in the evidence is limited for two reasons. Firstly, people who received monoclonal antibodies had more brain swelling and microbleeds than people receiving placebo. However, most studies did not separate people with symptoms of brain swelling and microbleeds from those in whom these effects were only visible with a scan. This reporting gap leaves patients without the information they need to understand the seriousness of potential unwanted effects. Secondly, the results came from studies that did not last very long. These are important limitations in the evidence for people with Alzheimer’s disease, who need to know the longer‐term benefits and unwanted effects of medicines.

We found six ongoing studies. The review's conclusions may change as new results become available.

How up to date is this evidence?

The evidence is current to 7 August 2025.

### Background

#### Description of the condition

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia, affecting 57.4 million people worldwide []. Epidemiological data in the last decade highlighted a sharp increase in AD incidence, which has increased from 26.6 million people worldwide in 2006, and is predicted to increase to about 106 million by 2050 []. Notably, 68% of the increase is expected to be localised in low‐ and middle‐income countries []. The disease is clinically characterised by progressive cognitive and functional decline, leading to progressive loss of independence and increasing care needs. The typical age of onset is over 65 years, and prevalence rates increase with increasing age [].

Clinically, AD evolves and is on a continuum, starting with a subjective report of cognitive decline followed by mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and progressing to mild, moderate, and severe dementia []. MCI due to AD is defined as objective cognitive decline (usually memory difficulties predominantly), while retaining independence in functional abilities []. MCI is often seen as a transitional stage between normal cognition and dementia. Among those with MCI, about 15% develop dementia after two years and about one third develop dementia due to AD within five years []. However, many individuals with MCI do not have additional cognitive decline or revert to normal cognition. A systematic review and meta‐analysis reported a reversion rate of 26% in population‐based studies []. In other words, MCI does not always lead inevitably to dementia, and recovery is possible in some cases [].

If dementia does develop, the next stage is mild dementia, which is characterised by cognitive decline that interferes with activities of daily living [].

While the diagnosis of “definite AD” can be performed only post‐mortem on neuropathological grounds, “possible” or “probable” AD and its severity staging can be diagnosed in living people, primarily by means of clinical evaluation and supported by neuroimaging techniques (magnetic resonance imaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) investigation, and amyloid positron emission tomography (PET)).

Two conceptual models—one linear and one nonlinear—have been proposed to explain the transition to clinical AD, but they remain theoretical and confined to research settings, and are not yet applied in routine clinical practice. Further exploration to understand their relevance to disease progression and possible reversibility is warranted [].

Biomarkers reflecting AD pathology, such as tau accumulation, measurable neurodegeneration, dynamic neurochemical alterations, can be detected in vivo in many healthy older persons without symptoms. These people will not all develop dementia. Therefore, the relevance of such biomarkers of disease without clinical symptoms is uncertain.

Both modifiable and non‐modifiable risk factors contribute to the development of AD. Non‐modifiable factors include age, genetic predisposition (particularly the presence of the APOE ε4 allele), and family history. Modifiable risk factors, representing potential targets for prevention strategies, include vascular risk factors (such as hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolaemia), low educational attainment, physical inactivity, smoking, depression, social isolation, and hearing loss [].

The pathological hallmarks of AD include extracellular deposition of aggregated amyloid‐beta protein (Aβ) and intracellular tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau protein [], along with synaptic and neuronal loss []. These changes lead to network dysfunction and progressive cognitive impairment. The first lesions are believed to start accumulating 10 to 15 years before the onset of cognitive symptoms.

The amyloid cascade hypothesis posits that amyloid‐beta accumulation is an early and pivotal event in AD pathogenesis, triggering downstream tauopathy, neurodegeneration, and clinical symptoms [].

Currently, no treatment is available to stop the natural course of AD towards progressive disability.

Current treatments for AD include drug therapies, such as cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine, and cognition‐oriented treatments. Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine exert a symptomatic action in people with AD by increasing the bioavailability of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine within cholinergic pathways disrupted by the disease and by slowing neurotoxicity processes, respectively. Both have a minimal to moderate effect [, , , ]. Cognition‐oriented treatments, including cognitive stimulation, cognitive training, and cognitive rehabilitation, are non‐pharmacological interventions aimed at slowing cognitive decline by engaging people with mild dementia in activities stimulating thinking, concentration, and memory [].

AD represents a major global health challenge, with a significant social and economic impact on health systems. Progressive loss of functional independence by people with AD implies an increasing need for support by caregivers, with a significant health and psychosocial impact on families and society. This burden is expected to increase with the ageing population and the rising prevalence of AD, placing a strain on healthcare systems.

#### Description of the intervention and how it might work

Based on the amyloid cascade hypothesis, amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies (Aβ‐mAbs) have been developed as potential disease‐modifying therapies for AD [], that is, they can potentially slow the disease, although they cannot cure it. The mechanism of action relates to binding to various forms of amyloid‐beta—monomeric, oligomeric, or fibrillar—and promoting clearance via microglial phagocytosis or peripheral mechanisms [, ]. The rationale behind the clinical use of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies is that reducing amyloid‐beta plaque burden and mitigating downstream neurodegenerative processes may slow cognitive and functional decline [].

Clinical trials have evaluated the effects of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies in people with MCI or mild dementia due to AD, that is, in the early symptomatic stages of AD when, it is hypothesised, amyloid‐beta‐targeting interventions might be most effective in slowing disease progression [].

Several clinical studies using repeated amyloid PET scanning have shown that the use of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies can lead to a reduction of aggregated amyloid‐beta in the brain, although the clinical impact of such findings is uncertain, with mixed results from RCTs, and poor correlation between amyloid removal and changes in cognitive function [, ].

First‐generation amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies targeting the monomeric amyloid‐beta, such as solanezumab, bapineuzumab, and crenezumab, failed to demonstrate clinical benefit for AD in clinical trials. In contrast, second‐generation amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies, including aducanumab, lecanemab, donanemab, and gantenerumab directed against pathogenic amyloid‐beta species and aggregates have shown mixed results, with some RCTs showing small differences in favour of the intervention arm on cognitive parameters after 18 months of treatment.

Amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies include aducanumab, bapinezumab, crenezumab, donanemab, gantenerumab, lecanemab, ponezumab, and solanezumab. They are administered intravenously, typically every two to four weeks, with dosage and administration protocols varying across agents. The approval by regulatory agencies of aducanumab [], donanemab [], and lecanemab [, ], represents a turning point in AD treatment. However, their net clinical benefit for people with AD is debated, with small effect sizes on cognition and function in pivotal trials paired with frequent occurrence of potentially serious adverse effects such as amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), including ARIA E, which refers to vasogenic oedema or effusions, and ARIA H, which indicates micro‐haemorrhagic events, as well as infusion reactions and other adverse events []. Moreover, some argue that the reported desirable effects may be the result of bias due to the functional unblinding that occurs when participants and investigators correctly guess whether a participant is receiving the active drug or placebo because of apparent adverse effects [, , ].

Patients at higher risk of developing ARIA include those carrying the APOE ε4 allele —especially homozygotes—as well as individuals with a high load of cerebral microbleeds at baseline, presumably reflecting amyloid angiopathy. Risk also appears to increase with higher antibody doses and more rapid titration schedules. These side effects can limit dosage and treatment duration [].

Furthermore, as mentioned, the drugs require repeated intravenous administration and close monitoring through neuroimaging assessments, with consequent organisational and economic implications adding to the cost of the medication.

#### Why it is important to do this review

The net benefit in clinical practice of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies is debated in the scientific community due to uncertainty after weighing the limited clinical relevance of efficacy outcomes against potentially severe adverse events, which are particularly frequent in subgroups of affected people.

The generalisability of results from RCTs on the efficacy and safety profile of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies across diverse health systems, particularly in relation to the requirements for their optimal and safe management, is currently debated []. The cost of these novel drugs, their administration, and the implementation of programmes to minimise the risk of adverse events may be challenging in many healthcare settings. The findings of the review could therefore be an important support for decision‐making in clinical practice.

Positions by health decision makers regarding amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies are discordant. Like the Food and Drug Agency and the European Medicines Agency, the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency approved lecanemab for MCI and mild dementia due to AD []. However, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence expressed a negative opinion about its reimbursement by the UK NHS (National Health System) since benefits are too small to justify the cost to the NHS []. The same opinion was expressed about donanemab []. Moreover, the Dutch Health Institute (Zorginstitut Nederland) refused reimbursement of lecanemab for patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD, based on the absence of net benefit. The Institute concluded that desirable effects on cognition are very small in the face of potentially serious side effects []. Recently, the German Institute for Quality and Efficiency in German Healthcare (IQWIG) concluded that lecanemab does not offer benefit to patients with MCI or early dementia due to AD [].

Numerous systematic reviews have been conducted on amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies for MCI and mild dementia due to AD, although none of them are Cochrane reviews. Their main limitations are being focused on only one agent [, ]; focusing only on efficacy [, ] or safety [, ]; methodological weaknesses [, ]; or including medications from drug classes other than amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies in pooled analysis []. Some review authors point out that, despite the statistical significance of results, whether the magnitude of their effect is clinically important remains uncertain [], while others report the absence of a positive effect [, ]. A recently published national guideline developed by means of the GRADE methodology strongly recommends against the use of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies [].

Given the rapidly evolving landscape of AD therapeutics and the significant implications for patients, caregivers, healthcare systems, and society, a comprehensive and up‐to‐date systematic review is particularly relevant now and is needed to appraise and synthesise current evidence on the efficacy and safety of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies in people with MCI or mild dementia due to AD.

### Objectives

To assess the clinical benefits and harms of the amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies aducanumab, bapineuzumab, crenezumab, donanemab, gantenerumab, lecanemab, ponezumab, remternetug, and solanezumab in people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease.

### Methods

We followed the Methodological expectations of Cochrane intervention reviews (MECIR) when conducting the review [], and PRISMA 2020 for the reporting [].

#### Differences between protocol and review

We modified our objectives to add remternetug to the list of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies.

We extracted the number of people with "any ARIA E" and "any ARIA H" separately, instead of extracting the number of "any ARIA" as stated in the protocol, since most of the included studies did not report the total number of people with ARIA.

#### Criteria for considering studies for this review

##### Types of studies

In this review, we included individual, parallel‐group, phase‐3 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and cluster‐RCTs of at least 12 months' duration that investigated amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies for the treatment of people with MCI or mild dementia due to AD. Phase‐3 RCTs can provide confirmatory evidence of efficacy and safety of interventions and are the most informative for clinical practice, regulatory decision‐making, and guideline development. Due to the clinical characteristics and the chronic, progressive course of AD, studies with follow‐up shorter than 12 months may not capture the benefits and harms of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies. Therefore, we excluded trials that lasted less than 12 months.

We did not include cross‐over trials since such a design is inappropriate for studies in AD due to the degenerative, progressive nature of the condition.

We included abstracts only if sufficient information was available on study design and characteristics of participants, interventions, and outcomes to allow extraction of data and evaluation of the risk of bias.

##### Types of participants

We included adult participants (aged 18 years or older) with a clinical diagnosis of:

- mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to probable or possible AD according to the criteria of the National Institute on Aging‐Alzheimer's Association workgroups on diagnostic guidelines for Alzheimer's disease [, , ] or of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition []; or
- mild dementia due to probable or possible AD according to the criteria of the National Institute on Aging‐Alzheimer's Association workgroups on diagnostic guidelines for AD [] or of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders fourth and fifth Edition [, ].

We included studies that had only a subset of eligible participants if data for the subgroup of interest were reported or were provided after we contacted the authors of the study. If we received no response from the study's authors within 15 working days of contacting them, we classified the study as 'awaiting classification'.

##### Types of interventions

Experimental intervention: treatment with any of the following amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies: aducanumab, bapineuzumab, crenezumab, donanemab, gantenerumab, lecanemab, ponezumab, remternetug, and solanezumab, at any dose and by parenteral route.

We allowed concomitant use of other medications, either as part of the study (e.g. in a factorial design) or as part of usual care, as long as the use in the intervention and control arm was the same.

Comparison intervention: placebo or no intervention

#### Search methods for identification of studies

##### Electronic searches

The Information Specialist identified eligible study references through systematic searches of the following bibliographic databases.

- The Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) (from inception to 2025, Issue 8)
- MEDLINE (PubMed) (from inception to 7 August 2025)
- Embase (Embase.com) (from inception to 7 August 2025)

We also searched two clinical trials registries: Clinicaltrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform [].

We did not apply any language restriction to the search. We used the Cochrane Highly Sensitive Search Strategy for identifying randomised trials for each bibliographic database, according to the methods recommended in Chapter 4 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions []. The most recent search date was 7 August 2025.

##### Searching other resources

To identify other relevant studies, we screened reference lists of review articles on the topic, as well as reference lists of included studies.

We examined any relevant retraction statements and errata for the included studies following the rules described in the Technical Supplement to Chapter 4: Searching for and selecting studies of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions []. We also checked the Retraction Watch Database.

Our preliminary search strategy (on MEDLINE‐PubMed database, see ) was adapted for use in the other electronic bibliographic databases.

#### Data collection and analysis

##### Selection of studies

Three pairs of review authors (from MGL, CB, CDS, LS, EB, LV) independently screened the citations and abstracts identified by the search. We used Covidence software for screening []. We retrieved full‐text reports for potentially eligible studies, and the same pairs of review authors independently screened them for eligibility. Two review authors (FN, GF) resolved any disagreements at both screening stages.

If necessary, we contacted study investigators to obtain or confirm relevant information concerning the inclusion and exclusion criteria. When there was no response from study investigators, we sent a reminder letter. When there was no response within 15 working days, we categorised the study as 'awaiting classification'.

We recorded the selection process in sufficient detail to complete a PRISMA flow diagram and a 'Characteristics of excluded studies' table.

##### Data extraction and management

We extracted data onto a pre‐designed form on a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. We piloted the form on four studies.

Two pairs of review authors (from MGL, SM, CDS, LV) independently extracted study information. The review authors were not blinded to authors, institutions, or journals. We resolved any disagreement about data extraction by referring to the study documents and discussing them as a review team. If necessary, we contacted the published trial authors to clarify or provide additional information.

We extracted the following study characteristics.

- Methods: setting, number of centres and location, dates, total duration, trial design, characteristics of trial design as outlined in
- Participants: number of participants randomised, mean age, age range, sex/gender, disease duration, severity of the condition, diagnostic criteria, cardiovascular disease as relevant comorbidity, inclusion and exclusion criteria
- Interventions: type of drug, doses, and frequencies, duration of treatment; comparison intervention, concomitant medications, and excluded medications
- Outcomes: critical and important outcomes, measures, and time points
- Notes: funding for trials and notable declarations of interest of trial authors
- Information needed to conduct GRADE assessments (e.g. baseline risk in the control group for key outcomes)

Two pairs of review authors (from MGL, SM, CDS, LV) independently extracted outcome data from the included studies.

We extracted the number of events and participants per treatment group for dichotomous outcomes, as well as means and standard deviations (SDs), and the number of participants per treatment group for continuous outcomes. We noted in the 'Characteristics of included studies' table if outcome data were not reported in a usable way, as well as when we transformed or estimated data from a graph. We resolved any disagreement about data extraction by referring to the study documents and discussing them as a review team.

We extracted the following outcome data.

- If both final and change‐from‐baseline values were reported for the same outcome, we extracted change‐from‐baseline data. If changes from baseline were not reported, we computed them by subtracting endpoint and baseline data, if provided.
- If both unadjusted and adjusted values for the same outcome were reported, we extracted the adjusted values.
- If available, we extracted data based on an intention‐to‐treat (ITT) sample.
- If multiple time points were reported, we extracted data from the latest time point reported in each of four time periods: 12 months, from above 12 months up to and including 18 months, from above 18 months up to and including 24 months, and above 24 months.

##### Risk of bias assessment in included studies

Two pairs of review authors (from MGL, SM, GF, EB) independently assessed the risk of bias of RCTs using the Cochrane risk of bias tool RoB 2 [], as described in Chapter 8 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions []. We resolved any disagreements by discussion.

This review focused on the effect of assignment to the intervention (i.e. the ITT effect).

For each included trial and each critical outcome, we assessed the following domains of bias.

- Bias arising from the randomisation process
- Bias due to deviations from intended interventions
- Bias due to missing outcome data
- Bias in measurement of the outcome
- Bias in selection of the reported result

We assessed the risk of bias for the following outcomes of critical importance that were reported in our summary of findings table: cognitive function measured by means of the ADAS‐CoG scale; dementia severity measured by means of the CDR‐SB scale; functional ability measured by means of the ACDS‐ADL, ACDS‐iADL, ACDS‐ADL‐MCI scales; and the following safety outcomes: any ARIA E and ARIA H, any symptomatic ARIA E and ARIA H, symptomatic brain haemorrhage, SAEs, mortality by any cause, at the end of treatment or the last follow‐up measurement time point, which was at 12 months, between above 12 months and 18 months, between above 18 months and 24 months, or above 24 months.

We judged each domain as 'low risk of bias', 'high risk of bias', or 'some concerns'. The judgements were facilitated by an algorithm that maps responses to the signalling questions, with options of 'yes', 'probably yes', 'probably no', 'no', and 'no information'. We presented an overall risk of bias judgement for each result based on our assessments across the five domains, which was no better than the worst risk of bias assessment in any one of the domains. We judged the overall risk of bias as 'low risk of bias', 'high risk of bias', or 'some concerns', according to the criteria in the current Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions []. We performed and documented the risk of bias assessment using the RoB 2 Excel tool.

We used our overall risk of bias assessment for each result in the review to inform our GRADE assessment (see ) and for sensitivity analysis (see ).

Had we found cluster‐RCTs, we would have used the RoB 2 tool for cluster‐randomised trials ( https://www.riskofbias.info/welcome/rob-2-0-tool/rob-2-for-cluster-randomized-trials ).

##### Measures of treatment effect

For dichotomous outcomes, we calculated risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For continuous outcomes, we estimated the standardised mean difference (SMD) with 95% CI.

##### Unit of analysis issues

When a single trial had multiple arms with different doses, we included only the highest dose in our analysis.

Had we found cluster‐RCTs, we would have analysed them according to methods described in Chapter 23 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions []. If studies did not control for clustering, we would have imputed the intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC) from similar studies. We would have divided the effective sample size of each arm by the 'design effect' quantity (1 + (M − 1) x ICC), where M is the average cluster size.

##### Dealing with missing data

For all outcomes, we carried out analyses on an ITT basis as far as possible. We examined the extent of and reasons for missing data as part of our risk of bias assessment for each included study. We did not contact trial authors to obtain any missing data. In order to assess the effect of missing outcome data, we also analysed dichotomous data according to a likely scenario, that is, we assumed that treated and control group participants who contributed to missing outcome data both had an unfavourable outcome (see ).

For dichotomous data, we used the number of participants analysed in the original study as a denominator.

For continuous outcomes, we calculated the SMD based on the number of participants analysed at that time point. If the number of participants analysed was not presented for each time point, we used the number of randomised participants in each group at baseline. Where possible, we calculated missing SDs from other statistics, such as standard errors, CIs, or P values. When none of the above methods allowed calculation of the SDs from the trial report, we borrowed the SD from one or more other studies, according to the methods recommended in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [].

##### Reporting bias assessment

When 10 or more studies were available for meta‐analysis, we investigated reporting biases (such as publication bias) by means of funnel plots. We made an assessment of funnel plot asymmetry visually.

##### Synthesis methods

We performed standard pairwise meta‐analyses for every comparison with at least two trials, using Cochrane's Review Manager (RevMan) software []. We used the random‐effects model because we assumed that the included studies were estimating different, yet related, intervention effects [].

We used the inverse‐variance approach, incorporating a measure of the extent of heterogeneity into the study weights. In particular, we used the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) to estimate the between‐study variance [].

As advised in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [], we calculated the CIs by means of the Wald‐type confidence interval method in the case of one or two studies and the Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman method in all other cases.

We conducted the following comparison: any amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibody at the highest assessed dose versus placebo or no intervention at the end of treatment or at the last follow‐up measurement.

We performed class‐level pooling across different amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies. We did this because all of them share a similar mechanism of action by targeting the Aβ, and amyloid‐PET imaging is the measure of target engagement for all of them. In spite of small differences in the epitope of the amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibody that is targeted, the effect of these drugs can therefore be evaluated as one class of drugs.

##### Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

We pooled data if studies were clinically homogeneous in respect of the study population, interventions, and outcomes. We assessed the presence of clinical heterogeneity within each pairwise comparison by comparing the trial and study population characteristics across all eligible trials. We assessed statistical heterogeneity in each meta‐analysis by visually inspecting the forest plot and using the Tau 2 , I 2 , and Chi 2 statistics. We did not calculate prediction intervals. We used the I 2 statistic to assess statistical heterogeneity, interpreting it as follows.

- 0% to 40% heterogeneity might not be important.
- 30% to 60% may represent moderate heterogeneity.
- 50% to 90% may represent substantial heterogeneity.
- 75% to 100% represents considerable heterogeneity.

These overlapping intervals reflect that the interpretation of the I 2 statistic depends on the size and direction of the treatment effect, and variance of the I 2 estimate. We did not pool results with considerable heterogeneity (I 2 of 75% or above).

We performed the following subgroup analyses for all critical and important outcomes with sufficient data.

- Type of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibody
- Level of cognitive function: MCI versus mild dementia (to be in the respective subgroups, studies had to have at least 70% of participants diagnosed at inclusion with MCI or mild dementia.)

We had also planned to perform subgroup analysis based on the comparator: placebo versus no treatment, but we did not do this because all studies compared treatment versus placebo.

We used the formal test for subgroup interactions in RevMan []. We interpreted subgroup analyses cautiously, as advised in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions []. We compared the magnitude of the effects between the subgroups by assessing the overlap of the CIs of the summary estimate, with non‐overlap of the CIs indicating statistical significance.

##### Equity‐related assessment

We assessed health equity by means of the PROGRESS framework [] and following guidance provided by the PRO EDI initiative (PRO EDI initiative) [, ]. We identified potentially overlooked populations by extracting recommended data in the participant characteristics table relative to age, sex/gender, race/ethnicity/ancestry, socioeconomic status, level of education, and location ().

We interpreted and discussed the availability of health equity data and their distribution across included studies under .

##### Sensitivity analysis

In order to assess the robustness of the synthesised results, we conducted the following sensitivity analyses for all critical and important outcomes.

- We included only study participants in whom PET or CSF testing for amyloid was abnormal (these may be entire study populations, or subgroups in mixed RCTs).
- We excluded studies at an overall high risk of bias.
- We analysed dichotomous data according to a likely scenario, that is, we assumed that treated and control group participants who contributed to missing outcome data both had an unfavourable outcome.

##### Certainty of the evidence assessment

We followed the guidelines in Chapters 14 and 15 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions for results [, ], taking care to distinguish a lack of evidence of effect from a lack of effect.

We created a summary of findings (SoF) table and presented the following critical outcomes measured at the end of treatment.

- Cognitive function measured by ADAS‐Cog
- Dementia severity measured by the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (CDR‐SB)
- Functional ability measured by the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study – Activities of Daily Living scale (ADCS‐ADL), the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study – Activities of Daily Living Mild Cognitive Impairment scale (ADCS‐ADL‐MCI), and the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study – Instrumental Activities of Daily Living scale (ACSD‐iADL)
- Any amyloid‐related imaging abnormality oedema (ARIA E)
- Symptomatic ARIA E
- Any amyloid‐related imaging abnormality haemorrhage (ARIA H)
- Symptomatic ARIA H
- Symptomatic brain haemorrhage
- Serious adverse events (SAEs)
- Any‐cause mortality

In SoF tables, we presented the comparison of any amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibody at the highest assessed dose versus placebo, reporting results at between 12 and 18 months, between above 18 months and 24 months, and above 24 months, depending on treatment duration of the studies. We present our main in the body of the review, and a second SoF table for time points above 18 months in .

We used the five GRADE domains (study limitations or overall risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias) to assess the certainty of a body of evidence as it relates to the studies that contributed data to the analyses of the prespecified outcomes; we reported the certainty of evidence as high, moderate, low, or very low [].

- Risk of bias: we considered the study's contribution (i.e. 'weight') to the pooled estimate. If more than 50% of the weight in the overall estimate came from studies at an overall high risk of bias, or if all the studies were judged as at overall unclear risk of bias, we downgraded the certainty of the evidence by one level. If more than 75% of the weight in the overall estimate came from studies at an overall high risk of bias, we downgraded by two levels.
- Imprecision: for each critical outcome, we assessed whether the CIs of the pooled estimates of absolute effect crossed predefined thresholds between trivial/no effect, small, moderate, and large effect, according to methods recommended in the Cochrane Handbook and by the GRADE Working Group guidance [, ]. Estimates suggesting a large effect with no apparent imprecision were further tested by assessing if the Review Information Size (RIS, also called Optimal Information Size, OIS) was met, and if not, we considered rating down for imprecision [, , ]. According to this approach, we downgraded the certainty of evidence by one, two, or three levels, depending on the number of thresholds that were crossed by the 95% CI of the absolute estimate of effect [, ]. For critical continuous outcomes, we computed the SMD to estimate the effect size, and our judgement about imprecision was based on 'Cohen’s d' thresholds for assessing the magnitude of effect (0.2 as threshold between trivial to small effect, 0.5 between small and moderate effect, and 0.8 between moderate and large effect) []. For critical dichotomous outcomes, in order to provide a numerical value of clinically important benefit, we defined thresholds between trivial/no effect, small effect, moderate effect, and large effect. For each outcome, we defined such thresholds by combining generic coefficients and the health state utility values (HSUVs) elicited from four clinicians with experience in dementia management (EB, LS, LV, ER) and three caregivers of people with dementia (see ) []. HSUVs are a summary index measure of health‐related quality of life used in economic analysis, usually obtained by means of surveys amongst people affected by a condition, asked to assign a value to health states on a scale on which 1 is equivalent to full health and 0 is considered equivalent to being dead []. Given the numerical imbalance between clinical experts and caregivers, we computed a weighted mean of the elicited HSUVs by attributing a weight of 1 to each one of the four clinicians and of 1.3 to each one of the three caregivers. See .
- Indirectness: we considered downgrading the certainty of the evidence by one level if the results were not fully generalisable to all countries/settings/participants; we did not downgrade by two levels for this domain.
- Inconsistency: we considered the statistical test for heterogeneity (I 2 ), overlap of CIs, and similarity of point estimates. We considered downgrading by one level if I 2 was substantial (between 50% and 75%) and the point estimates of individual studies were in different directions.
- Publication bias: as described in the section, when we included 10 or more studies in a meta‐analysis, we evaluated potential publication bias by means of funnel plots. We assessed funnel plot asymmetry visually. If publication bias was suggested by a visual assessment, we downgraded the evidence certainty by one level [].

The GRADE approach uses the following definitions of the certainty of the evidence.

- High: we are very confident that the true effect lies close to that of the estimate of the effect.
- Moderate: we are moderately confident in the effect estimate: the true effect is likely to be close to the estimate of the effect, but there is a possibility that it is substantially different.
- Low: our confidence in the effect estimate is limited: the true effect may be substantially different from the estimate of the effect.
- Very low: we have very little confidence in the effect estimate: the true effect is likely to be substantially different from the estimate of effect.

Two review authors (FN, SM) independently assessed the certainty of the evidence, with any disagreements resolved by discussion or by involving a third review author (GF). We used GRADEpro GDT software to prepare the SoF tables []. We reported and justified all decisions to downgrade the certainty of the evidence in footnotes. When pooled analysis was not possible due to high heterogeneity, the assessment of certainty of the evidence across the five GRADE domains (methodological limitations of the studies or risk of bias, indirectness, imprecision, inconsistency, and the likelihood of publication bias) was considered narratively by two review authors (FN, SM) [].

###### Study designs and settings

All 17 included studies were RCTs with parallel‐group randomisation. We did not find any cluster‐RCTs. The 17 studies included a total of 20,342 participants.

The studies were conducted between 2014 and 2024, with durations varying from 18 months to 27 months. The studies were performed in multicentre settings, and took place in different countries, including Argentina, Australia, Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Bulgaria, Canada, Chile, Costa Rica, Croatia, Czech Republic, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Hong Kong, Hungary, Italy, Japan, Lithuania, Mexico, Netherlands, New Zealand, Poland, Portugal, Republic of Korea, Russia, Serbia, Slovakia, Slovenia, South Africa, South Korea, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Turkey, Ukraine, the UK, and the USA.

###### Participants

An overview of the characteristics of the included participants is provided in and .

The mean age of the participants varied from 69.5 years [] to 73.9 years []. The percentage of female participants in the studies ranged from 50% [] to 64.5% []. Gender identity was not reported in any study, although the terms 'sex' and 'gender' were frequently used interchangeably to refer to biological sex.

The distribution of cognitive subtypes varied across studies. Seven studies enroled only participants with mild dementia [, , , , , , ], and one study included only participants with MCI []. In the remaining nine studies with a mixed population, the distribution ranged from 15.7% MCI and 84.3% mild dementia [] to 83% MCI and 17% mild dementia []. One study did not report the distribution [].

The mean duration of cognitive impairment ranged from 16.8 ± 18 months [] to 51.6 ± 31.2 months [].

No study reported participants' cardiovascular disease comorbidities. Fifteen studies (88.2%) included participants with concomitant use of donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, or memantine. One study explicitly excluded participants requiring memantine or acetylcholinesterase inhibitors [], and one study did not provide information about this [].

Race, ethnicity, or ancestry were comprehensively reported in 14 studies (82.4%). Three studies reported only the proportion of white participants [, , ], and one study mentioned race in the methods without reporting any data []. Across the studies, the mean percentage of white participants was 83.8%. No study presented a mean percentage of white participants below 75%, and four studies reported more than 90% white participants [, , , ]. Ethnicity categorised as Hispanic/Latino versus non‐Hispanic/Latino was reported in six studies [, , , , , , ], with reported mean values ranging from 2% [] to 23.7% [].

No study reported markers of socioeconomic status.

Mean years of participant education ranged from 11.8 ± 3.8 [] to 14.7 ± 3.7 []. Five studies (23.5%) did not provide information on participants’ educational level [, , , , ].

###### Interventions and comparisons

Three studies assessed aducanumab [, , ]; four assessed bapineuzumab [, , , ]; two assessed crenezumab [, ]; one assessed donanemab []; four assessed gantenerumab [, , , ]; one assessed lecanemab []; and two assessed solanezumab [, ].

All studies compared the active medication with a placebo group.

#### Risk of bias in included studies

We assessed the risk of bias in the outcomes that we reported in our summary of findings table, using the RoB 2 tool []. The results of our assessment can be found in the RoB 2 tables () and on the forest plots of the analyses. Detailed assessments, including our responses to the signalling questions, are available from the corresponding author (FN) on reasonable request. Below, we provide a summary of the assessment.

Overall, our assessment of the risk of bias was very similar across all the included studies.

For four studies [, , , ], we evaluated the overall risk of bias as "some concerns" due to the randomisation process domain, as the methods for random sequence generation or allocation concealment (or both) were not described.

All of the studies were double‐blind and provided an adequate description of the methods used to guarantee blinding of participants, providers, and outcome assessors; however, for the efficacy outcomes, we judged the overall risk of bias as "some concerns" for all studies, due to bias in measurement of the outcomes, as adverse effects (i.e. infusion reaction) were much more frequent in the treated arm, which could lead to functional unblinding. We judged the safety outcomes ARIA E and ARIA H to be at low risk of bias for measurement of the outcomes, as the radiologists who read the MRIs were blind to treatment assignment in all the studies. We also judged the safety outcomes of serious adverse events and overall mortality to be at low risk of bias for measurement of the outcomes, as the outcomes were objective and unlikely to be biased by functional unblinding.

For three studies [, , ], we evaluated the overall risk of bias as "some concerns" due to selection of the reported results, as they did not provide a statistical analysis plan.

##### Equity assessment

We assessed equity using the PROGRESS Plus framework. Reporting of equity‐relevant characteristics in the studies was inconsistent, with notable gaps in the description of trial populations. Age, location, and education were generally reported, but no study reported markers of socioeconomic status. The sex of participants was reported in all studies. However, the terms "sex" and "gender" were often used interchangeably, referring to biological sex only. Although most studies reported information on race, white participants consistently represented more than 75% of the studies' populations, while black participants and Hispanic/Latino ethnic groups were markedly under‐represented.

Furthermore, assessment of eligibility for treatment with amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies requires genetic testing and determination of the amyloid load in the brain by investigation of cerebrospinal fluid or amyloid PET scan. Once the treatment is started, monitoring with repeated MRI scans of the brain is required to detect the occurrence of ARIA. Such investigations, particularly in settings with limited resources, may be challenging for health systems and create inequities by limiting access to treatment.

Overall, the available data offer only partial insight into non‐white populations and into patterns of equitable access to treatment, highlighting areas where future research and reporting may be strengthened.

### Discussion

#### Summary of main results

We found 17 eligible placebo‐controlled phase‐3 RCTs on therapy with amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies in MCI or mild dementia due to AD. Most studies investigated the efficacy of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies on cognitive function, severity of dementia, and functional ability (critical outcomes of our review) by means of continuous measures (differences in the scale scores between intervention and control groups). Dementia severity and functional ability were not assessed in all studies. To allow comparison between different continuous outcomes and the contextualisation of their pooled estimates in terms of magnitude of the effect, we expressed all pooled estimates as SMDs. Undesirable events were reported as dichotomous outcomes (number of people presenting the event), and we assessed differences between intervention and control groups as relative risks. Here, we mainly discuss the results at 18 months because treatment lasted 18 months in most studies, so using this time point allowed us to provide more representative and precise estimates.

##### Cognitive function

The most widely used tool to measure cognitive function was the ADAS‐Cog scale (range 0 to 70 points). Across 13 studies with a duration of 18 months, the pooled SMD between the intervention and the placebo arms was probably trivial, being below the Cohen's d value of 0.2. In absolute terms, the pooled observed difference in favour of the treatment on the ADAS‐COg scale at 18 months was 0.85 points (moderate certainty). The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for ADAS‐Cog, according to anchor‐based approaches, is generally considered to be 2 to 3 points in the MCI stage and 4 points in the dementia stage over a 6‐ to 12‐month period [, , ]. The ADAS‐Cog difference found in the RCTs included in this review is therefore considerably lower over a longer period of time. At longer time points, estimates may be similar, with absolute differences of 1.41 points at 24 months, but the evidence is very uncertain (very low certainty), and of 1.27 points at more than 24 months (low certainty).

##### Dementia severity

Dementia severity was assessed at 18, 24, and above 24 months by means of the CDR‐SB scale (range 0 to 18 points). The pooled SMD at 18 months across nine studies may be trivial. In absolute terms, the observed estimate translates into a reduction of 0.29 points (low certainty). The MCID for the CDR‐SB scale, according to anchor‐based approaches, is generally considered to be 1 point in the MCI stage and 2 points in the dementia stage []; therefore, the observed difference does not seem to be clinically meaningful. Similar results were observed at 24 months and above 24 months.

##### Functional ability

Functional ability was assessed by means of various scales, with most studies using the ADCS‐ADL (range 0 to 78 points) and its variants, ADCS‐iADL (range 0 to 56 points) and ADCS‐MCI (range 0 to 53 points).

At 18 months, a pooled SMD probably in favour of treatment but of trivial magnitude was observed (moderate certainty). Small SMD values were observed in studies where functional ability was assessed at 18 months by means of the ADCS‐ADL‐MCI scale (4 studies) and ADCS‐iADL scale (1 study) (low certainty for both findings). Although no established MCID exists for such scales, absolute differences like those observed, ranging from 0.07 to 1.90 points, do not suggest meaningful clinical improvements on scales with such score ranges.

##### Other outcomes

Behavioural symptoms were assessed by means of the NPI‐Q scale in three studies, and no differences between treatment and control groups were found at 18 months. No clear conclusions can therefore be drawn on the effect of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies on behavioural symptoms.

##### Adverse events

###### ARIA

The most common undesirable events associated with the use of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies are ARIA, which at onset can be either symptomatic or, more commonly, asymptomatic. Symptomatic ARIA are particularly threatening, given the potentially severe neurological consequences and the need for immediate assessment and management. Nevertheless, ARIA with asymptomatic onset may also have long‐term undesirable consequences, which are currently uncertain. The reporting of ARIA was inconsistent across studies. Only four studies specifically reported symptomatic ARIA E, and only one study reported symptomatic ARIA H. At 18 months, ARIA (particularly ARIA E) were more common in the treatment groups than in the control groups. Specifically at 18 months, amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies probably resulted in a small absolute increase of any ARIA E and in a trivial increase in the risk of symptomatic ARIA E (moderate certainty for both findings). Overall, at 18 months, ARIA H were probably no more common amongst people treated with amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies than in people receiving a placebo, based on the three studies that reported such data (moderate certainty). However, these studies showed high heterogeneity, and it was not possible to pool their results. Amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies probably resulted in a trivial increase of symptomatic ARIA H (moderate certainty).

Estimates at the longest available follow‐up could be more informative about long‐term safety in a chronic condition like AD. Above 24 months, the pooled estimate of any ARIA E may have suggested a large difference in absolute terms (low certainty), while the estimate of ARIA H probably showed a small increase (moderate certainty). Symptomatic ARIA E may have shown a trivial absolute difference compared to placebo (low certainty), and no data were available on symptomatic ARIA H above 24 months.

APOE ε4 allele homozygosis is associated with a higher risk of developing ARIA. Therefore, we considered a subgroup analysis on APOE ε4 homozygotes versus non homozygotes or non carriers. However, we did not perform it because genetic testing for APOE ε4 status is not part of usual clinical practice, given the substantial impact of test results on patients' offspring, and most of the included studies did not report APOE ε4 status.

###### SAE and mortality

SAEs and mortality showed trivial differences between the groups at 18 months (high certainty), as well as at the other time points.

#### Limitations of the evidence included in the review

Although the populations investigated in the included studies match the target population of our review (adults with MCI or mild dementia due to AD), the external validity of the study results for the wider AD population is limited. The mean age of trial participants varied between 69.5 and 73.9 years, whereas estimates outside the experimental setting report the mean age of onset to be over 80 years []. Due to the strict inclusion criteria, estimates of the AD population potentially eligible for this treatment range from 5% to 15% [, ].

We found notable heterogeneity amongst studies in the outcomes of efficacy and in the tools that the study authors used to assess them. Moreover, the timing of the outcome assessment varied, with only six of the 17 studies considering follow‐up duration longer than 18 months [, , , , , ], which would be desirable in a chronic condition like AD. Such limitations make it challenging to make comparisons between different amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies and to calculate precise pooled estimates, and they hamper the generalisability of the results.

Safety is an important issue when using amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies in clinical practice. An important limitation of the available published literature is the lack of data on symptomatic ARIA, information that is particularly relevant for patients and doctors. Surprisingly, only four studies reported the number of people with symptomatic ARIA E [, , , ], and only one study reported the number of people with symptomatic ARIA H [].

Including observational cohorts or phase‐IV studies in future reviews may provide further information about the sustained use of the medication and about the risk of adverse events, including ARIA E and ARIA H. Given the relatively short follow‐up in available studies and considering the long disease course of AD, more information on the long‐term safety profile of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies is needed.

Although our assessment of risk of bias across domains found no limitations in the randomisation procedures, we had some concerns with respect to efficacy outcome measures, the magnitude of which may have been substantially impacted by functional unblinding. In some studies (Analysis 8.1; Analysis 8.2), participants in the amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies groups experienced side effects considerably more often than in the placebo groups, causing symptoms or prompting changes in the treatment regimen that could lead to unblinding of study participants and their caregivers to their allocated treatment. Such functional unblinding may have influenced outcome measures of cognition, dementia severity, and functional ability, hampering the validity of such estimates. In particular, for outcomes that are partly informant‐based, such as the CDR‐SB, functional unblinding could have biased the result towards a more positive rating. An attempt to quantify this bias was made by Wolters and colleagues [], but the analyses were based on three studies [, , ] and cannot be directly extrapolated to all 17 studies included in our review. Because of the trivial differences in efficacy outcomes between treatment and placebo arms observed across trials, any impact of functional unblinding could further attenuate this difference towards the null. For this reason, while implementing the GRADE methodology, we downgraded our assessment of the certainty of efficacy outcomes by one level for the risk‐of‐bias domain.

All included studies were funded by the pharmaceutical industry, which, in most cases, had control over crucial stages, such as the analysis and interpretation of data, and writing of the final manuscript. In some studies, all co‐authors were employees of the company manufacturing the drug being tested (see for more details). Sponsor‐related influence on the reported results, particularly in relation to the lack of data on ARIA, cannot be excluded and deserves further investigation in future reviews by means of specific tools.

AD is a very common disorder. In several of the trials, the total number of participants included per participating centre was low, suggesting possible selection bias. Most studies included predominantly white populations in Europe and North America, limiting the external validity for people of other ethnicities.

Another limitation of the evidence on amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies is the lack of extractable data on the education level of participants, which would be relevant information, particularly regarding the outcome of cognitive function.

For most of the undesirable outcomes, we downgraded the certainty of the evidence by one or two levels for imprecision because the 95% CIs of the point estimates crossed one or more thresholds between different magnitudes of effect.

#### Limitations of the review processes

We used the methods recommended by the Cochrane Handbook and did not identify any major limitation in the review process.

#### Agreements and disagreements with other studies or reviews

The small‐to‐absent effects of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies against placebo urge us to reconsider the amyloid cascade hypothesis as the main underlying mechanism of AD. According to this hypothesis, amyloid deposition is a relatively early step in a cascade towards eventual neurodegeneration and the occurrence of symptoms. This would imply that if the therapy was not effective, the pathology may have been too advanced, and interventions should take place even before clinical symptoms occur, which, in turn, would suggest preventive treatment of amyloid‐positive persons without symptoms. Major ethical and societal concerns would arise, and the potential number of eligible individuals would be enormous.

Such a phase‐3 RCT in asymptomatic amyloid‐PET‐positive people has been done with solanezumab, with an intervention and follow‐up period of 240 weeks []. No difference was observed between the solanezumab arm and the placebo arm on scales for cognition, activities of daily living, and severity, despite there being less amyloid build‐up in the solanezumab arm. This is in line with a previous meta‐analysis of RCTs on amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies for AD that showed no association between successful amyloid removal and cognitive function [].

Moreover, a phase‐3 RCT in autosomal dominantly inherited AD comparing gantenerumab or solanezumab to placebo during 4.7 years of treatment did not show benefit for treated patients, in spite of effective amyloid removal from the brain [].

Several systematic reviews have been developed on the efficacy and safety of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies for AD. Albeit with some inconsistency in methods (inclusion/exclusion criteria, type of analysis, and reporting), the results of recently published reviews with objectives that are similar to those of our review also show relatively small efficacy estimates for cognitive and functional outcomes, together with a higher number of people with ARIA, particularly ARIA E, mostly asymptomatic [, ].

### Authors' conclusions

#### Implications for practice

The effect of amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies (Aβ‐mAbs) in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or early dementia due to Alzheimer's disease (AD) on cognitive decline, dementia severity, and functional outcomes over an 18‐month period, is either absent or consistently small. The differences in the main clinical outcome measures, including ADAS‐Cog (Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive) and CDR‐SB (Clinical Dementia Rating Scale Sum of Boxes) scores, are well below the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) on these scales. There are some concerns that the true effect size may be even smaller because of functional unblinding due to common apparent adverse reactions. The risk of adverse events, most notably amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), is high. Although symptomatic ARIA is uncommon, the long‐term consequences of cerebral oedema and recurrent (micro) bleedings are uncertain. The absence of clear reporting of symptomatic ARIA in most studies is a main limitation that precludes a thorough analysis of the true impact of ARIA. The burden on healthcare systems for these treatments, with subsequent inequities in access to care, may be considerable due to the additional resources required for intravenous (IV) administration of the drugs, PET (positron emission tomography) scans, and repeated MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) scans to monitor for ARIA, in addition to the cost of the drug.

Given the small effect sizes, with absolute differences well below the MCID, the high risk of ARIA, and the substantial resource burden required for their administration and monitoring, monoclonal antibodies against amyloid‐beta do not offer a favourable benefit to risk balance for patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD.

##### Equity‐related implications for practice

As described above, assessment of eligibility for treatment with monoclonal antibodies against amyloid‐beta requires determination of the amyloid load in the brain by investigation of cerebrospinal fluid or amyloid PET scan. Once the treatment is started, monitoring with repeated MRI scans of the brain is required to detect the occurrence of ARIA. Such investigations, particularly in settings with limited resources, may be burdensome for health systems and create inequities by limiting access to treatment. These challenges may not allow replication of the estimates of efficacy and safety of this class of drugs outside the experimental setting. The use of these drugs seems to be a realistic option only in high‐resource health systems, and even in these settings, the required resources (PET‐scanning, repeated MRI scanning, nursing staff for frequent IV treatment) may not be available to an extent that can ensure equitable access to treatment.

#### Implications for research

Future studies should report more transparently on the impact of ARIA. From the current published results, including supplementary materials, in most studies it is not possible to truly know the clinical impact of ARIA. Cerebral oedema and cerebral (micro‐)bleeds are serious conditions and a clear interpretation of the impact on people with AD should be reported in all trials.

The current results make clear that successfully removing amyloid from the brain probably does not result in a clinically meaningful effect on cognitive decline in patients with MCI or early dementia due to AD. New mechanisms of action for treatment of AD, other than aiming for amyloid removal, should be explored. This may include many different approaches, for example, reducing inflammation or investigating the significance of the gut‐brain axis [].

Given the convincing evidence of the absence of a clinically meaningful effect, in combination with a considerable risk of cerebral oedema and (micro)bleeds in the brain, from the 17 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) included in this review, it is unlikely that future RCTs targeting amyloid‐beta removal will provide clear benefit for people with AD.

In order to ensure generalisability of results, future research on drug treatments for AD should collect data on determinants impacting baseline risk of populations included in the trials, particularly modifiable ones such as socioeconomic status [].

Future studies should prioritise clinically meaningful outcomes. Sample size calculations should be based on MCIDs, wherever available. Intervention and follow‐up periods should be longer, given the slow rate of decline in the MCI and early dementia stage of AD. More transparent reporting on the occurrence of ARIA and their clinical manifestations (at a minimum, whether or not they are symptomatic) is warranted. While an open‐label extension of an RCT is not suitable to estimate clinical efficacy, it can be useful to determine the long‐term risk of adverse events and to detect rare but severe adverse events that may go unnoticed in relatively short RCTs.

##### Equity‐related implications for research

The limited and inconsistent reporting of equity‐related characteristics and the under‐representation of non‐white populations may restrict the assessment of differential treatment effects across groups at highest risk of AD [, ]. The inconsistent reporting of educational attainment and the lack of socioeconomic status indicators highlights the need for improved equity‐informed trial design and reporting, in order to consider social determinants in future research on disease‐modifying treatments for AD.
