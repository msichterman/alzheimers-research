When a doctor suspects Alzheimer's disease or mild cognitive impairment (MCI), they order a sequence of tests. In the past, diagnosis was largely based on eliminating other causes. Today, biological markers can identify the underlying disease process with high precision.

Here is what families need to know about each test, what it measures, and what questions to ask your doctor.

## 1. Cognitive and Memory Assessments

Before any high-tech scan, clinicians evaluate memory, language, reasoning, and problem-solving abilities.

* **Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE) & MoCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment):** 10-to-30 minute pencil-and-paper or verbal tests measuring orientation, short-term recall, and executive function.
* **Comprehensive Neuropsychological Testing:** Multi-hour testing administered by a neuropsychologist to detect subtle patterns differentiating Alzheimer's from vascular changes, depression, or frontotemporal degeneration.

## 2. Blood Biomarker Tests (e.g., p-tau217, p-tau181)

Blood tests for Alzheimer's represent the newest major development in clinical care.

* **What they measure:** Specific phosphorylated tau proteins (like **p-tau217**) and amyloid ratios in plasma. Elevated p-tau217 correlates strongly with amyloid buildup in the brain.
* **Accuracy:** Recent clinical studies (such as the 2024 JAMA APS2 study) show high-performance blood tests achieve 90%+ diagnostic accuracy in clinical settings.
* **Role in care:** Blood tests are primarily used as triage tools to decide whether confirmatory imaging or specialty referral is required.

:::tip Questions to Ask Your Doctor About Blood Tests
* *"Is this test FDA-cleared or an established clinical laboratory test?"*
* *"Does an elevated result mean a definitive diagnosis, or do we need a confirmatory scan?"*
* *"Are there factors (like kidney function) that might influence my test result?"*
:::

## 3. Brain Imaging: MRI and PET Scans

Brain imaging visualizes brain structure as well as microscopic protein buildup.

### MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging)
* **Purpose:** Uses magnetic fields to produce detailed pictures of brain tissue.
* **What doctors look for:** Shrinkage (atrophy) in the hippocampus (the memory center), evidence of past silent strokes, vascular disease, or normal pressure hydrocephalus.
* **Safety & Baseline:** MRI is also required before starting anti-amyloid antibody treatments (Leqembi or Kisunla) to screen for baseline microbleeds.

### Amyloid and Tau PET Scans (Positron Emission Tomography)
* **Purpose:** A safe radioactive tracer is injected into the bloodstream, binding specifically to amyloid plaques or tau tangles in the brain.
* **What it proves:** Confirms whether amyloid plaque burden is present in the brain cortex, establishing eligibility for amyloid-targeting therapies.

## 4. Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Analysis / Lumbar Puncture

A lumbar puncture (spinal tap) collects fluid surrounding the spinal cord and brain.

* **What it measures:** Direct concentrations of Aβ42, Aβ40, total tau, and p-tau181.
* **When it is used:** Often used in specialty memory clinics when blood tests or PET scans are inconclusive, or when atypical symptoms are present.
