With the recent FDA approvals of disease-modifying therapies, families have more options than ever—along with important decisions regarding benefits, safety risks, and appointment scheduling.

Here is an objective overview of how current treatments work, what they achieve, and who qualifies.

## Disease-Modifying Therapies (Anti-Amyloid Antibodies)

Unlike older medications that only manage symptoms temporarily, disease-modifying therapies target the biological root cause of Alzheimer's by clearing amyloid plaques from the brain.

### 1. Leqembi (lecanemab)
* **Approval Status:** Traditional FDA approval (July 2023).
* **How it is given:** Intravenous (IV) infusion every 2 weeks (or maintenance dosing options).
* **What clinical trials showed:** Slowed clinical decline on the CDR-SB cognitive scale by approximately **27% over 18 months** in patients with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) or mild Alzheimer's dementia with confirmed amyloid pathology.

### 2. Kisunla (donanemab)
* **Approval Status:** FDA approval (July 2024).
* **How it is given:** IV infusion every 4 weeks.
* **Special feature:** Treatment may be stopped once brain amyloid plaques have been completely cleared below a specific threshold on PET scans, potentially reducing treatment burden over time.
* **What clinical trials showed:** Slowed clinical decline by approximately **22% to 29%** across primary cognitive and functional rating scales over 76 weeks.

:::important Qualification Criteria
These therapies are approved specifically for **early-stage disease** (MCI or mild dementia) with documented brain amyloid. They are **not** approved or effective for moderate or severe dementia stages.
:::

## Understanding Safety: What is ARIA?

The primary safety consideration with all anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies is **ARIA** (Amyloid-Related Imaging Abnormalities).

* **ARIA-E (Edema):** Temporary swelling or fluid accumulation in areas of the brain.
* **ARIA-H (Hemorrhage):** Small microbleeds or superficial siderosis on the brain's surface.

### Key ARIA Facts
1. **Most cases are asymptomatic:** Many patients with ARIA experience no symptoms, and the changes are detected only on routine surveillance MRIs.
2. **Potential symptoms:** Headache, confusion, dizziness, visual changes, or nausea.
3. **Monitoring schedule:** Frequent MRI scans (typically before infusions 5, 7, and 14) are required by the FDA to monitor safety.
4. **Genetic risk (APOE4):** Individuals carrying two copies of the APOE-ε4 gene have a higher risk of developing ARIA, so genetic testing is recommended prior to initiation.

## Symptom-Managing Medications

For moderate-to-severe symptoms or alongside disease-modifying drugs, physicians frequently prescribe traditional symptomatic medications:

* **Cholinesterase Inhibitors (Donepezil, Rivastigmine, Galantamine):** Boost levels of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter critical for memory and thinking.
* **NMDA Receptor Antagonists (Memantine / Namenda):** Regulate glutamate activity to protect brain cells from overstimulation; often used in moderate-to-severe stages.
* **Combination Therapies (Namzaric):** Donepezil + Memantine combined.

## Questions to Discuss with Your Neurologist

1. *Does our confirmed biomarker status (PET or CSF) meet the criteria for monoclonal antibody treatment?*
2. *What is the infusion and MRI monitoring schedule for our local clinic?*
3. *What is our APOE genotype, and how does it impact our risk of ARIA?*
4. *Are any medications being taken (especially blood thinners) that might increase bleeding risks?*
