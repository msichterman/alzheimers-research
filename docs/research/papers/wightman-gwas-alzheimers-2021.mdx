## Takeaway

This meta-analysis provides robust genetic evidence that microglia and immune dysfunction drive late-onset Alzheimer's disease. It identified 38 independent risk loci, including seven previously unreported regions. However, translating these polygenic modifiers into successful clinical therapies remains a significant hurdle.

## Key findings

- Identified 38 independent genomic risk loci for late-onset Alzheimer's disease, including seven newly discovered regions (paper, Results).
- Demonstrated that Alzheimer's-associated gene expression is highly enriched in microglia and immune cells, supporting a neuroinflammatory mechanism (paper, Results).
- Linked genes previously associated primarily with frontotemporal dementia, such as *TMEM106B* and *GRN*, directly to late-onset Alzheimer's risk (paper, Results).

## Methods in brief

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) meta-analysis of 1,126,563 individuals. The cohort included clinical cases, proxy cases based on parental history, and controls. The population was primarily of European descent.

## Trial evidence

While this genetic data strongly implicates microglial pathways, clinical translation has proven difficult. The most advanced test of this hypothesis—[Alector's TREM2 agonist AL002](/trials/al002-invoke-2-nct04592874)—demonstrated target engagement but failed to show clinical benefit in its Phase 2 trial. Similarly, programs targeting the newly implicated progranulin (*GRN*) pathway have struggled, with the leading drug for frontotemporal dementia recently terminating in Phase 3.

## Public sentiment

Public perception is sharply divided by community. Scientific audiences praise the unprecedented sample size and the definitive validation of the microglial hypothesis. Conversely, patient and caregiver communities exhibit 'treatment fatigue' regarding new genetic discoveries. They express frustration over the lack of immediate therapies and frequently confuse these subtle, polygenic risk modifiers with deterministic, early-onset genes.

## Caveats

- The inclusion of 'proxy cases' based solely on parental history of Alzheimer's introduces potential phenotype misclassification, as these individuals are not medically verified.
- The study cohorts and reference panels were overwhelmingly of European descent, limiting the applicability of the genetic findings to other global populations.
- Statistical fine-mapping cannot definitively pinpoint causal genes due to linkage disequilibrium, meaning experimental validation is still required for the identified loci.
