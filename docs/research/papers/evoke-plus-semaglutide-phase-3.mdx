## Takeaway

Oral semaglutide failed to slow cognitive decline in early Alzheimer's disease. The Phase 3 EVOKE Plus trial missed its primary clinical endpoint at week 104, ending the drug's prospects as a symptomatic treatment.

## Key findings

- Enrolled 1,840 adults with early Alzheimer's disease to test up to 14 mg of daily oral semaglutide ([NCT04777409](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04777409)).
- Failed to show a significant difference from placebo in CDR-SB scores at week 104 ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42261705/)).

## Methods in brief

A Phase 3, randomized, quadruple-masked, placebo-controlled trial. It enrolled 1,840 adults aged 55-85 with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia of the Alzheimer's type and confirmed amyloid positivity. Participants received up to 14 mg of oral semaglutide daily, with the main phase lasting 104 weeks ([NCT04777409](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04777409)).

## Trial evidence

The clinical evidence base for treating early Alzheimer's disease with oral semaglutide is definitively negative. Both EVOKE Plus and its sister trial, EVOKE, failed to meet their primary efficacy endpoint of slowing cognitive decline measured by the CDR-SB at week 104. The sponsor declared the program a failure, a conclusion corroborated by peer-reviewed literature ([Lancet](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42594906/)).

## Public sentiment

Public perception is highly polarized. Retail investors reacted negatively, causing significant stock drops following the top-line trial failures ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/siladityaray/2025/11/24/novo-nordisk-stock-plummets-after-ozempic-makers-alzheimers-drug-trial-fails/)). Conversely, patients and caregivers often maintain hope, conflating the drug's well-established metabolic benefits with dementia protection. Scientific discourse has shifted from symptomatic treatment to debating the drug's potential for early prevention.
