**Source**: [The Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD016297) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41985900)
**Authors**: Nonino, F. et al. — Cochrane Collaboration

## Takeaway

Despite effectively clearing amyloid plaques, anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies produce statistically significant but clinically trivial improvements in cognitive and functional decline. Given the tenfold increased risk of brain edema and bleeding, these therapies currently exhibit an unfavorable benefit-to-risk profile for early Alzheimer's disease.

## Key findings

- Cognitive function (ADAS-Cog) showed a trivial standardized mean difference of -0.11 at 18 months, translating to a 0.85-point absolute benefit, well below the 2-4 point clinical significance threshold (paper, Summary of findings 1).
- Dementia severity (CDR-SB) demonstrated a similarly negligible benefit with a 0.29-point absolute reduction at 18 months (paper, Summary of findings 1).
- Risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities involving edema (ARIA-E) was ten times higher in treated patients, representing an absolute increase of 107 events per 1,000 participants (paper, Summary of findings 1).
- No significant differences were observed in overall mortality or serious adverse events between treatment and placebo arms at 18 months (paper, Summary of findings 1).

## Methods in brief

Systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 phase-3 randomized controlled trials evaluating 20,342 adults with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to probable or possible Alzheimer's disease. Primary analyses evaluated outcomes after 18 months of treatment.

## Trial evidence

This review aggregates data across the entire class of anti-amyloid antibodies, anchoring the clinical evidence record. It includes pivotal successes like [Clarity AD](/trials/clarity-ad-nct03887455) and [TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2](/trials/trailblazer-alz-2-nct04437511), as well as terminated programs such as [EMERGE](/trials/emerge-nct02484547)/[ENGAGE](/trials/engage-nct02477800) and [GRADUATE I](/trials/graduate-1-nct03444870). The pooled data robustly support that while recent trials met primary statistical endpoints, the absolute clinical differences remain consistently marginal across the class.

## Public sentiment

Public perception is starkly divided. Patient and caregiver communities on platforms like Reddit express desperate optimism, often interpreting these drugs as disease-modifying breakthroughs while minimizing ARIA risks. Conversely, researchers and the biotech community remain highly skeptical, highlighting the disconnect between biological plaque clearance and actual cognitive improvement, and questioning the drugs' commercial valuations given the severe risk-to-benefit profile.

## Caveats

- Most primary studies failed to cleanly separate symptomatic from asymptomatic ARIA, making the true clinical burden of brain swelling difficult to quantify.
- Noticeable side effects like ARIA and infusion reactions likely caused functional unblinding in the trials, potentially biasing subjective cognitive assessments in favor of the treatment.
- The 18-month primary follow-up across most trials is relatively short for a chronic, slowly progressing neurodegenerative disease.
- Trial populations were overwhelmingly white (averaging 84%) and lacked socioeconomic diversity, limiting the generalizability of the findings to broader populations.

### Bias & spin flags

- Potential functional unblinding: Noticeable side effects (ARIA, infusion reactions) in the active treatment arms may have unblinded participants and assessors, positively biasing subjective outcome scales like CDR-SB.
- The systematic failure of primary study authors to clearly report the clinical manifestations of ARIA (i.e., whether they were symptomatic) leaves a crucial gap in understanding the true severity of the adverse effects.
