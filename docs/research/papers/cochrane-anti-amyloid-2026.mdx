## Critical review
**Primary endpoint: not_met.** The review's critical efficacy outcomes at 18 months (ADAS‐Cog SMD −0.11, CDR‐SB SMD −0.12, ADCS‐ADL SMD 0.09) were judged trivial and the corresponding absolute differences fall well below the minimal clinically important differences the review cites for these scales.
> The differences in the main clinical outcome measures, including ADAS‐Cog (Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive) and CDR‐SB (Clinical Dementia Rating Scale Sum of Boxes) scores, are well below the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) on these scales.
**Multiplicity handling:** none stated — the review describes no adjustment for multiple comparisons; it states only that subgroup analyses were interpreted cautiously using a formal test for subgroup interactions

### Risk of bias

- **randomisation process** — some concerns for four studies, because methods of random sequence generation or allocation concealment (or both) were not described
- **measurement of the outcome — functional unblinding (efficacy outcomes)** — some concerns for all studies for the efficacy outcomes, because adverse effects were much more frequent in the treated arm and could cause functional unblinding; the certainty of efficacy outcomes was downgraded one level for risk of bias
- **measurement of the outcome — safety outcomes** — low risk of bias for ARIA E, ARIA H, serious adverse events and overall mortality, because MRI readers were blinded and the safety outcomes were objective
- **selection of the reported result** — some concerns for three studies, which did not provide a statistical analysis plan
- **overall risk of bias by outcome** — low overall risk of bias for serious adverse events and mortality; some concerns for efficacy outcomes, mainly due to functional unblinding
- **high risk of bias studies / sensitivity analysis** — only one included study was at overall high risk of bias; excluding it gave pooled estimates consistent with the primary analysis
- **sponsor involvement / industry funding of included trials** — all included trials were industry funded, with sponsors controlling analysis, interpretation and writing in most cases; sponsor‐related influence on results, particularly regarding missing ARIA data, cannot be excluded
- **publication bias** — funnel plots for the two outcomes with at least 10 studies did not suggest significant publication bias on visual inspection

### Limitations stated by the document

- Both desirable outcomes and adverse events were inconsistently reported in the studies included in the review.
- Most studies did not separate people with symptoms of brain swelling and microbleeds from those in whom these effects were only visible with a scan; only four studies reported symptomatic ARIA E and only one reported symptomatic ARIA H.
- The results came from studies that did not last very long; only six of the 17 studies had follow‐up longer than 18 months, which limits knowledge of longer‐term benefits and harms.
- External validity is limited: mean age of trial participants was 69.5 to 73.9 years whereas mean age of onset outside the experimental setting is reported to be over 80 years, and only an estimated 5% to 15% of the AD population would be eligible for treatment.
- Notable heterogeneity among studies in efficacy outcomes and in the tools used to assess them, and variation in the timing of outcome assessment, hampering precise pooled estimates and generalisability.
- Some concerns about risk of bias for efficacy outcomes due to functional unblinding, which may mean the true effect size is even smaller.
- All included studies were funded by the pharmaceutical industry, which in most cases controlled analysis, interpretation of data and writing of the manuscript; sponsor‐related influence cannot be excluded.
- In several trials the number of participants per participating centre was low, suggesting possible selection bias; most studies included predominantly white populations in Europe and North America.
- Lack of extractable data on the education level of participants, and no study reported markers of socioeconomic status.
- For most undesirable outcomes the certainty of evidence was downgraded by one or two levels for imprecision.
- APOE ε4 subgroup analysis was planned but not performed because most included studies did not report APOE ε4 status.
- Six ongoing studies were identified and the review's conclusions may change as new results become available.
- We used the methods recommended by the Cochrane Handbook and did not identify any major limitation in the review process.

### Where authoritative sources disagree

*Stored as structured evidence with each position attributed to its source, not resolved into a single verdict.*

- **Is successful removal of amyloid from the brain associated with clinically meaningful benefit in people with MCI or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's disease?** — The review authors conclude that successful amyloid removal does not appear to produce clinically meaningful effects, and that future research on disease‐modifying treatment should target other mechanisms of action. *(Cochrane review authors (Nonino and colleagues))*
- **Are the observed differences on cognitive and functional scales clinically important?** — The review authors hold that the effects over 18 months are absent or consistently small and that the differences on ADAS‐Cog and CDR‐SB are well below the minimal clinically important difference for those scales. *(Cochrane review authors)*
- **Do amyloid‐beta‐targeting monoclonal antibodies offer a favourable benefit‐to‐risk balance, as implied by the regulatory approvals of aducanumab, donanemab and lecanemab?** — The review authors conclude that, given small effect sizes below the MCID, the high risk of ARIA and the resource burden, these antibodies do not offer a favourable benefit to risk balance — a position the review contrasts with the approvals granted by the FDA, EMA and MHRA while noting NICE, the Dutch Health Institute and IQWiG reached negative assessments. *(Cochrane review authors)*
- **Is the amyloid cascade hypothesis tenable as the main underlying mechanism of Alzheimer's disease?** — The review authors argue that the small‐to‐absent effects observed require the amyloid cascade hypothesis to be reconsidered as the main underlying mechanism of AD. *(Cochrane review authors)*
- **Could the reported efficacy of these drugs be inflated by functional unblinding?** — The review authors state there are concerns that the true effect size may be even smaller than reported because common apparent adverse reactions can unblind participants and investigators, and that this bias could attenuate the difference further towards the null. *(Cochrane review authors)*
- **Should further randomised trials of amyloid‐beta removal be conducted?** — The review authors judge that, given convincing evidence of the absence of a clinically meaningful effect together with the risk of cerebral oedema and microbleeds, future RCTs targeting amyloid‐beta removal are unlikely to provide clear benefit. *(Cochrane review authors)*
