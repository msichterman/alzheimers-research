**Source**: [ClinicalTrials.gov, 2021](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01206062) · Primary outcomes published in [NEJM, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1511939) and SPRINT-MIND in [JAMA, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.21442)
**Authors**: SPRINT Research Group — National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI)

## Takeaway

Targeting a systolic blood pressure below 120 mm Hg significantly reduces major cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in high-risk older adults without diabetes. While the SPRINT-MIND substudy found a reduction in mild cognitive impairment (MCI), the intervention did not significantly reduce the incidence of probable dementia. The cardiovascular and cognitive benefits must be weighed against increased risks of hypotension, syncope, and acute kidney injury.

## Key findings

- Intensive blood pressure control reduced major cardiovascular events compared to standard control (243 vs 319 events; HR 0.75, p&lt;0.001) (registry, Primary Outcome).
- All-cause mortality was significantly lower in the intensive arm (155 vs 210 deaths; HR 0.73, p=0.003) (registry, Secondary Outcome).
- The intervention did not significantly reduce the incidence of all-cause dementia over the observed timeframe (149 vs 176 cases; HR 0.83, p=0.10) (registry, Secondary Outcome).
- Adverse events including hypotension (48 vs 27 cases), syncope (56 vs 33), and acute kidney injury (11 vs 3) were more frequent in the intensive arm (registry, Adverse Events).

## Methods in brief

A randomized, single-masked interventional trial enrolling 9,361 adults aged 50 and older with elevated cardiovascular risk, excluding those with diabetes or prior stroke. Participants were assigned to intensive (SBP &lt;120 mm Hg) or standard (SBP &lt;140 mm Hg) blood pressure targets. The intervention was stopped early after a median follow-up of 3.3 years due to overwhelming cardiovascular benefit.

## Trial evidence

SPRINT provides exceptionally strong evidence for the cardiovascular benefits of intensive blood pressure control in non-diabetic adults, prompting widespread guideline updates. The [ACCORD trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00000620) provides critical context, having previously demonstrated that this specific robust cardiovascular benefit does not replicate in patients with diabetes. The early termination of SPRINT likely reduced the statistical power needed to definitively measure slower-developing outcomes like all-cause dementia.

## Public sentiment

Public sentiment is a mix of enthusiasm from health media and caution from geriatricians. Major news outlets frequently cite the trial as proof that intensive blood pressure control prevents dementia, occasionally conflating Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) with Alzheimer's disease. Caregiver communities express hope but also confusion regarding prevention versus disease reversal. Meanwhile, scientists and physicians emphasize the need to balance cognitive benefits against the tangible risks of falls, syncope, and kidney injury in frail, older patients.

## Caveats

- The trial was stopped early due to clear cardiovascular benefit, which inherently truncates the follow-up time needed to adequately assess slower-developing endpoints like dementia.
- Findings specifically apply to patients without diabetes or prior stroke; previous trials have shown these intensive targets do not yield the same benefits in diabetic populations.
- The intensive blood pressure target carries genuine risks of hypotension, syncope, and acute kidney injury that must be carefully managed in clinical practice.
