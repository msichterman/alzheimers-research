**Source**: [Nature Medicine, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-02049-x) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36357681)
**Authors**: Rik Ossenkoppele et al.

## Takeaway

Cognitively unimpaired individuals with both amyloid and tau PET positivity face a significantly higher risk of short-term clinical progression (3–5 years) to mild cognitive impairment and dementia. The risk is exceptionally high—over 40-fold—when tau pathology has spread to the neocortex. These findings validate the biological definition of preclinical Alzheimer's disease and provide a critical stratification tool for early-intervention trials.

## Key findings

- Individuals with neocortical tau (A+T NEO-T+) progressed to MCI at 19.2 times the rate of biomarker-negative peers (paper, Abstract).
- Progression to all-cause dementia was 41.3 times higher for the A+T NEO-T+ group compared to the reference group (paper, Abstract).
- Cognitive decline in tau-positive groups was significantly faster on global cognitive measures than in groups with amyloid alone or negative biomarkers (paper, Results).

## Methods in brief

This multicenter longitudinal observational study followed 1,325 cognitively unimpaired individuals from seven cohorts for an average of 41.8 months. Participants were grouped by amyloid and tau PET status, specifically differentiating between tau in the medial temporal lobe (MTL) versus the neocortex (NEO-T).

## Trial evidence

This study provides robust observational evidence linking PET biomarkers to rapid clinical decline, supporting the biological definition of preclinical Alzheimer's disease. However, predicting decline is only part of the clinical puzzle. The failure of the [A4 Study](/research/papers/solanezumab-a4-prevention) (solanezumab) in a similar population means early detection does not yet guarantee an actionable preventative treatment. The field awaits readouts from trials of highly potent plaque-clearing therapies like [AHEAD 3-45](/trials/ahead-3-45) and [TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 3](/trials/trailblazer-alz-3) to see if early intervention can halt this predicted progression.

## Public sentiment

Public perception is sharply divided. The scientific community largely celebrates the validation of the biological cascade and its utility for clinical trial design. In contrast, patient and caregiver communities express deep skepticism about early testing. They cite the psychological burden of a preclinical diagnosis without an accessible, curative treatment to alter the disease course.

## Caveats

- The study establishes relative risk over a short follow-up (~3.5 years), not absolute lifetime risk, and does not account for competing risks like mortality.
- The group with isolated tau positivity (A-T+) was too small for main analyses, limiting insights into tau's impact independent of amyloid.
- Differences in PET tracers, cognitive tests, and recruitment strategies across the seven participating cohorts may introduce variability into the pooled findings.

### Cross-examination

3 facts verified against the source document and trial registries. Flagged items:

- **Fact check (disputed)** — Media claims that PET scans diagnose Alzheimer's 'decades' before symptoms. The study's actual average follow-up was 3.5 years. It establishes group-level short-term clinical progression risk, not a multi-decade lifetime prediction.
- **Registry cross-check** — The document's implication that defining the A+T+ unimpaired group as having 'preclinical Alzheimer's disease' constitutes an actionable medical state must be carefully contextualized: while the predictive value of these biomarkers for progression is observationally validated by the registry data, the only completed Phase 3 trial targeting this specific preclinical population to date (A4 Study) failed to alter clinical progression.
- **Misinformation watch** — The circulating claim that a positive amyloid and tau scan in a healthy person guarantees they will develop clinical dementia, ignoring that the study establishes relative risk over a short period and does not account for competing lifetime risks (such as dying of other causes).
