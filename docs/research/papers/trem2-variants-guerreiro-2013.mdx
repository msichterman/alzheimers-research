## Takeaway

This landmark 2013 study established rare loss-of-function variants in the TREM2 gene—most notably R47H—as major genetic risk factors for late-onset Alzheimer's disease. It demonstrated that TREM2 expression increases and localizes to microglia surrounding amyloid plaques, cementing the role of neuroinflammation in disease pathology.

## Key findings

- The R47H variant confers roughly a five-fold increased risk of Alzheimer's disease (odds ratio 5.05) (paper, RESULTS).
- Variants in TREM2 exon 2 were significantly more frequent in Alzheimer's patients (22 of 1092) than in controls (5 of 1107) (paper, Abstract).
- In transgenic mouse models, Trem2 expression increased as amyloid accumulated and localized specifically to microglia around amyloid plaques (paper, RESULTS).

## Methods in brief

Case-control genetic association study using exome and genome sequencing of 1,092 Alzheimer's patients and 1,107 controls. Findings were replicated via meta-analysis of GWAS data and direct genotyping in an independent cohort of 1,887 cases and 4,061 controls. The researchers also analyzed TREM2 mRNA expression in human brains and a transgenic mouse model.

## Trial evidence

While this paper validated TREM2 as a major genetic risk factor, translating this into therapy has proven difficult. Alector's TREM2-activating antibody AL002 showed target engagement in Phase 1 but failed to slow cognitive decline in the Phase 2 [INVOKE-2](/trials/al002-invoke-2-nct04592874) trial for early Alzheimer's. This disparity suggests that targeting a lifelong genetic risk factor may not be an effective therapeutic strategy once clinical symptoms begin.

## Public sentiment

Public perception is divided by community. Scientists and biotech investors initially viewed TREM2 as a highly promising 'beyond amyloid' target, though recent trial failures have tempered enthusiasm. Meanwhile, awareness of TREM2 remains virtually nonexistent among patients and caregivers, who focus largely on daily disease management and approved therapies.

## Caveats

- Identifying a lifelong genetic risk factor does not guarantee that pharmacologically modulating the target later in life will alter the disease trajectory.
- Microglial activation is highly dynamic; therapies that boost the immune response might be protective early on but risk driving harmful neuroinflammation during later disease stages.
