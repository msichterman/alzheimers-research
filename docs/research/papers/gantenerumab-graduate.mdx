## Critical review
**Primary endpoint: not_met.** The primary outcome — change from baseline in CDR-SB at week 116 — was not met in either trial: the difference was −0.31 (95% CI, −0.66 to 0.05; P = 0.10) in GRADUATE I and −0.19 (95% CI, −0.55 to 0.17; P = 0.30) in GRADUATE II, and because the hierarchical testing failed at the primary outcome all subsequent outcomes were considered not significant.

### Risk of bias

- **sponsor design, funding and analysis** — The trials were designed and funded by the sponsor, the sponsor supplied drug and placebo, sponsor-employed authors analyzed the data, and sponsor-funded medical writers assisted with the manuscript; the document states the sponsor could not interdict or delay publication and that authors vouch for data completeness and accuracy.
- **unblinding via ARIA** — The document reports specific measures to protect blinding (CDR raters unaware of group assignment and shielded from ARIA data, separate dosing personnel, blinded neuroradiologists) and reports sensitivity analyses concluding the primary findings did not result from unblinding of safety data.
- **power and enrollment shortfall** — The power calculation assumed approximately 1016 participants per trial group across both trials combined, while 1965 participants total were enrolled; the document also states pandemic-related missed visits would reduce power from 90% to 80%.
- **protocol amendment during conduct** — The double-blind treatment period was extended from 104 to 116 weeks by protocol amendment during the trials in response to Covid-19-related missed visits.
- **missing data and imputation** — Primary and secondary outcomes used conditional mean imputation with reference-based (copy-increment-from-reference) assumptions for intercurrent events deemed related to the trial drug or condition, with an independent adjudication committee classifying those events.
- **attrition/discontinuation imbalance** — Discontinuation due to an adverse event was markedly more frequent with gantenerumab (9.1%) than placebo (1.8%), driven by protocol-specified discontinuation criteria for ARIA-H.
- **generalizability** — The document states the trial population from the United States lacked racial diversity, which may affect generalizability, while also asserting that sex, age, and race/ethnic distribution indicates representativeness.

### Limitations stated by the document

- The lack of racial diversity in the trial population from the United States, which may affect the generalizability of the findings
- The multiple differences between the protocol for these trials and the protocols for earlier trials of anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies (including the previously untested dose-escalation scheme), which make it difficult to draw comparisons between trials
- The trials did not assess the hypothesis that rapid plaque reduction, probably to a level below the threshold of detection, may be necessary to show clinical efficacy within 18 to 27 months
- The pooled analysis of both trials was not part of the hierarchical analysis and no definitive conclusions can be drawn from it
- No definitive conclusions can be drawn from the post hoc exploratory analysis of clinical response among participants attaining amyloid-negative status
- The Covid-19 pandemic caused delayed and missed visits, estimated at a mean of 8 weeks of the double-blind treatment period, reducing power from 90% to 80% and requiring a protocol amendment extending treatment to 116 weeks

### Where authoritative sources disagree

*Stored as structured evidence with each position attributed to its source, not resolved into a single verdict.*

- **Does gantenerumab slow clinical decline in early Alzheimer's disease?** — It does not. Gantenerumab lowered amyloid plaque burden relative to placebo at 116 weeks but was not associated with slower clinical decline, and secondary clinical outcomes were not supportive of a beneficial clinical effect. *(Bateman et al., the GRADUATE I and II authors (this document))*
- **Did gantenerumab remove as much amyloid plaque as prior trials and modeling predicted?** — No. The magnitude of amyloid plaque removal was smaller than expected on the basis of previous trials and prespecified modeling predictions, and mean amyloid level on PET remained elevated after treatment. *(Bateman et al., the GRADUATE I and II authors (this document))*
- **Is rapid, near-complete plaque reduction required for anti-amyloid antibodies to show clinical efficacy?** — The document advances this as a hypothesis generated by these results taken with other anti-Aβ antibody trials, while explicitly stating the current trials did not test it. *(Bateman et al., the GRADUATE I and II authors (this document))*
- **Do the greater brain-volume decreases and ventricular increases seen with gantenerumab indicate neurodegeneration?** — They may not reflect neurodegeneration, because amyloid plaque removal and fluid shifts could affect brain volume; the findings are similar to those in other amyloid-targeting trials. *(Bateman et al., the GRADUATE I and II authors (this document))*
- **Are ARIA-E and amyloid plaque removal driven by the same mechanism?** — Probably not; observed ARIA-E incidence matched the predicted 25% while plaque removal was lower than predicted, which the authors say suggests different mechanisms drive ARIA-E and plaque removal. *(Bateman et al., the GRADUATE I and II authors (this document))*
- **Is there a treatment effect on brain tau accumulation?** — No. There was no appreciable between-group difference in tau on PET in any of the four composite regions, despite lower CSF and plasma phosphorylated tau 181 with gantenerumab; the authors suggest the absence may be due to limited amyloid plaque removal. *(Bateman et al., the GRADUATE I and II authors (this document))*
