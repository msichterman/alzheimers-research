## Takeaway

The 2026 pipeline is dominated by disease-modifying therapies and heavily integrates biomarkers for trial eligibility. While the review highlights a large volume of repurposed agents in Phase 2, this optimism must be balanced against high-profile Phase 3 failures of repurposed drugs that occurred shortly after this snapshot.

## Key findings

- The pipeline includes 158 candidate drugs in 192 active trials, a 40% increase over the past decade (paper, Abstract).
- Disease-targeted therapies (DTTs) comprise 73% of the pipeline, evenly split between small molecules and biologics (paper, Results).
- Biomarkers are now standard practice, featuring in 87% of Phase 3 and 89% of Phase 2 trials for eligibility or outcome tracking (paper, Results).
- Repurposed drugs represent 35% of the pipeline, primarily driven by non-industry sponsors in earlier clinical phases (paper, Results).

## Methods in brief

Systematic review of the clinicaltrials.gov registry to identify active interventional trials for Alzheimer's disease across all phases and disease stages, indexed as of January 1, 2026.

## Trial evidence

As an annual pipeline review, this document maps the landscape rather than reporting new efficacy data. It underscores the clinical shift toward disease-modifying treatments following the successes of anti-amyloid biologics. However, the clinical evidence context reveals that the large "repurposed" pipeline segment is highly vulnerable, as demonstrated by the failure of semaglutide in the Phase 3 EVOKE trials shortly after this review was published.

## Public sentiment

Public perception of the pipeline is deeply polarized. Mainstream media and institutions frequently frame the pipeline's growth and recent approvals as historic breakthroughs. In contrast, patient and retail investor communities exhibit profound skepticism, weighed down by the high costs, intensive safety monitoring requirements for ARIA, and the ultimately modest clinical benefits of currently approved therapies.

## Caveats

- The pipeline snapshot is static as of January 1, 2026; major trial readouts occurring later in the year render some of its optimism—particularly around repurposed drugs—outdated.
- The analysis relies exclusively on clinicaltrials.gov, meaning trials conducted solely outside North America may be omitted if they are not registered on the US platform.
- Classifying therapies as disease-modifying versus symptomatic relies on the sponsors' stated therapeutic intent rather than proven mechanistic outcomes.

### Cross-examination

4 facts verified against the source document and trial registries. Flagged items:

- **Registry cross-check** — The document's pipeline snapshot as of January 2026 treats massive repurposed drug programs (like semaglutide in EVOKE) as active candidates, but recent 2026 readouts definitively show they failed their Phase 3 primary endpoints, making the viable repurposed pipeline smaller than the review implies.
- **Registry cross-check** — The authors explicitly count 192 'active' trials for AD, whereas an unfiltered query of ClinicalTrials.gov for active interventional AD trials yields almost 800 results, indicating the authors applied heavy, subjective filtering criteria (e.g., excluding non-pharmacological, device, or dietary supplement interventions) not strictly transparent in the raw counts.
- **Misinformation watch** — The pervasive belief that anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies (like Leqembi or Kisunla) are a 'cure' rather than a treatment that modestly slows decline.
- **Misinformation watch** — Circulating claims on patient forums that all beta-amyloid targeting therapies are 'conclusive' failures, ignoring recent FDA approvals and trial data.
- **Misinformation watch** — The perception by some desperate caregivers that legitimate, biomarker-driven clinical trials are financial scams due to their complex and restrictive enrollment procedures.
