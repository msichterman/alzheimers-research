## Takeaway

This foundational preclinical study identified "Disease-Associated Microglia" (DAM), a specialized cellular state that clusters around amyloid plaques and phagocytoses them. It demonstrated that transitioning to this protective state requires the TREM2 receptor, a finding that sparked massive biopharma investment in TREM2-activating drugs.

## Key findings

- Researchers identified a novel subset of microglia, termed DAM, by mapping immune populations in wild-type and Alzheimer's transgenic mouse brains.
- DAMs colocalize with amyloid plaques and contain intracellular Aβ particles, suggesting a phagocytic role.
- The activation of the DAM program is a two-step process: an initial Trem2-independent downregulation of microglial checkpoints, followed by Trem2-dependent full activation.

## Methods in brief

Single-cell RNA sequencing and immunohistochemical analysis of wild-type mice, Alzheimer's transgenic (Tg-AD) mice, Trem2-knockout (Trem2−/−) Tg-AD mice, and human brain slices.

## Trial evidence

While this paper positioned TREM2-dependent DAMs as a highly promising therapeutic target to restrict neurodegeneration, clinical translation has proven difficult. The first major test of this hypothesis in humans, the TREM2 agonistic antibody [AL002](/trials/al002-invoke-2-nct04592874), failed to slow cognitive decline in a Phase 2 trial and increased amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA).

## Public sentiment

Perception of this hypothesis is sharply divided. The scientific and investment communities (e.g., driving Sanofi's acquisition of Vigil Neuroscience) enthusiastically embraced TREM2 agonists as a potential paradigm shift. However, commercial discourse frequently oversimplifies DAMs as universally protective, overlooking emerging scientific nuance that these microglia can become senescent or harmful over time.

## Caveats

- The mechanistic description of the two-step DAM activation relies almost exclusively on transgenic mouse models, which may not accurately reflect human Alzheimer's pathophysiology.
- Subsequent clinical failures of TREM2 agonists strongly suggest that pharmacologically forcing this microglial state in humans does not safely or effectively restrict neurodegeneration.
