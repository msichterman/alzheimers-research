## Critical review
**Primary endpoint: not_met.** The primary efficacy end point — change from baseline to week 73 on the CDR-SB — was not met: increases were similar in placebo (Δ = 2.19) and all three semorinemab arms (Δ = 2.36, 2.36 and 2.41), with no differences at any post-dosing time point; the co-primary safety outcome was descriptive and the document reports an acceptable and well-tolerated safety profile.
> semorinemab did not slow clinical AD progression compared with placebo throughout the 73-week study period but did demonstrate an acceptable and well-tolerated safety profile
**Multiplicity handling:** none stated — the document describes no method of controlling for multiple comparisons; it states only that the study was powered at the α = .20 level for the 4500-mg versus placebo CDR-SB comparison

### Risk of bias

- **liberal type I error rate / statistical power** — The study was designed to yield 80% power at a two-sided-unspecified α = .20 significance level for a single dose comparison (4500 mg vs placebo), a far more permissive threshold than conventional α = .05, and the document describes no multiplicity control across the three dose arms and multiple endpoints.
- **attrition** — 19% of participants discontinued during the blinded portion of the study, and 35 of 457 randomized participants were not included in the mITT efficacy cohort (457 randomized, 422 analysed); the document states discontinuation was similar across treatment arms.
- **COVID-19 disruption of exposure and assessment** — 72 participants missed 112 planned doses, and CDR, ADCS-ADL and A-IADL-Q were administered telephonically for 13% of week-69 and 16% of week-73 CDRs, introducing exposure and measurement heterogeneity that sensitivity analyses could only partly address.
- **internal numerical inconsistency between abstract and Table 2** — The abstract reports adverse event counts that equal the arm denominators (placebo 130 [93.1%]; 4500 mg 132 [94.7%]; 8100 mg 90 [92.2%]) whereas Table 2 reports 121 (93.1%), 124 (93.9%) and 83 (92.2%); the counts and one percentage disagree between the two locations.
- **sponsor involvement in conduct and analysis** — The document refers to the sponsor as a blinded party to treatment allocation and describes the preclinical work supporting semorinemab as the authors' own ('Our preclinical studies'), but the retrieved text contains no funding statement or conflict-of-interest disclosure.
- **generalizability of the study population** — Of 422 participants in the mITT cohort, 388 (91.9%) were White, which the document itself flags as restricting generalizability.

### Limitations stated by the document

- The COVID-19 pandemic contributed to missed doses and/or remote or missed clinical assessments for some participants; targeted sensitivity analyses yielded results similar to the overall analyses, but more subtle pandemic-related effects may have been obscured
- Relatively few longitudinal CSF samples were available for analyses of tau indices, limiting the ability to determine whether semorinemab-related reductions in CSF tau were dose-dependent and/or related to clinical outcomes
- The limited racial and ethnic diversity among trial participants may restrict the generalizability of the findings

### Where authoritative sources disagree

*Stored as structured evidence with each position attributed to its source, not resolved into a single verdict.*

- **Does semorinemab slow clinical decline or cerebral tau accumulation in prodromal to mild Alzheimer disease?** — It does not. Semorinemab failed to demonstrate meaningful efficacy on clinical end points or reduction of tau accumulation across 18 months at the doses administered, and the authors present this as a negative result rather than an inconclusive one. *(Teng et al., the Tauriel investigators (this document))*
- **Was the null result attributable to insufficient disease progression in the trial population?** — No. Decline on CDR-SB and tau PET accumulation were comparable with prior reports, so failure to detect a treatment effect was not due to insufficient progression. *(Teng et al., the Tauriel investigators (this document))*
- **Do these results refute the tau-spreading hypothesis of AD pathogenesis?** — The authors explicitly decline to draw that conclusion, stating their results do not directly address the validity of the tau-spreading hypothesis. *(Teng et al., the Tauriel investigators (this document))*
- **Why did preclinical efficacy of the murine surrogate not translate to the clinic?** — The document offers several unresolved explanations, including that the N-terminal tau epitope semorinemab recognises may not be present in the tau species responsible for spread, and that tau may spread by membrane-enclosed mechanisms that preclude antibody clearance. *(Teng et al., the Tauriel investigators (this document))*
- **Are other N-terminal anti-tau antibodies effective in analogous populations?** — The document reports that similarly negative results for gosuranemab and tilavonemab in analogous patient populations have recently been announced, while maintaining that tau remains a compelling therapeutic target. *(Teng et al., the Tauriel investigators (this document))*
