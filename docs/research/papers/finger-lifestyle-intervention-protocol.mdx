## Takeaway

The FINGER trial protocol established the framework for testing whether a combination of nutritional guidance, exercise, cognitive training, and vascular risk monitoring can prevent cognitive decline. While this registry record lacks data, the protocol laid the foundation for landmark peer-reviewed findings demonstrating the efficacy of multi-domain lifestyle interventions in at-risk older adults.

## Key findings

- The registry record outlines a 2-year intervention comparing multi-domain lifestyle counseling to standard health counseling (ClinicalTrials.gov).
- The primary prespecified endpoint was cognitive impairment measured by a Neuropsychological Test Battery (NTB), Stroop, and Trail Making tests (ClinicalTrials.gov).
- Although the registry indicates 'Results posted: false', subsequent peer-reviewed publications reported significant cognitive benefits (Clinical-trials researcher).

## Methods in brief

Interventional, randomized, single-blind trial aiming to enroll 1,200 older adults (aged 60-77) with an elevated dementia risk (Dementia Risk Score >= 6) and specific cognitive deficits. The primary intervention phase lasted 2 years. Registered as NCT01041989.

## Trial evidence

The FINGER protocol represents a foundational proof-of-concept in dementia prevention. Although the ClinicalTrials.gov record is poorly maintained (listed as 'Unknown' status with no results posted), the [FINGER trial](/trials/finger-nct01041989) was successfully completed and read out in 2015, showing that multi-domain interventions significantly preserve cognitive function. This success spawned the World-Wide FINGERS network and directly inspired replication studies like the [US-POINTER trial](/trials/us-pointer-nct03688126).

## Public sentiment

Public sentiment regarding the FINGER protocol and lifestyle interventions is predominantly hopeful, particularly in tech and science communities that emphasize actionable, non-pharmacological prevention. However, media hype often oversimplifies the evidence, conflating the modest slowing of cognitive decline in asymptomatic, at-risk individuals with a 'cure' for established Alzheimer's disease. Caregiver communities remain notably quiet on the protocol, often expressing exhaustion over unsolicited lifestyle advice for patients who are already severely impaired.

## Caveats

- This document is a prospective registry protocol, not a peer-reviewed paper, and contains no empirical results or data.
- The clinical registry record is officially abandoned or poorly maintained, showing an 'Unknown' status despite the trial completing its primary phase in 2013.
- Public discourse frequently loses the vital nuance that this intervention aims to slow cognitive decline in 'at-risk' populations, not reverse established clinical dementia.

### Cross-examination

2 facts verified against the source document and trial registries. Flagged items:

- **Fact check (corrected)** — Enrollment of 1260 participants. The ClinicalTrials.gov source document estimates enrollment at 1,200. The 1,260 figure comes from the final published Lancet paper, not the registry itself.
- **Registry cross-check** — The document correctly notes that no empirical data or results are posted directly on the ClinicalTrials.gov registry for NCT01041989 and that its status is listed as 'UNKNOWN'; however, this reflects poor registry maintenance rather than a lack of actual data, as the study's highly successful primary outcomes were peer-reviewed and published in The Lancet in 2015.
- **Misinformation watch** — The false claim that diet changes and cognitive puzzles (sudoku) can reverse or cure established, late-stage clinical Alzheimer's disease.
- **Misinformation watch** — The belief that lifestyle modifications completely neutralize all genetic risk factors (like APOE-e4 homozygosity).
