**Authors**: Clifford R. Jack et al. — National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA)

## Takeaway

The 2018 NIA-AA framework radically shifted Alzheimer's research by defining the disease biologically using the AT(N) classification (amyloid, tau, neurodegeneration). Under this system, "Alzheimer's disease" requires biomarker evidence of both amyloid and pathologic tau, treating cognitive impairment as a downstream symptom for staging rather than the disease itself.

## Key findings

- 10% to 30% of patients clinically diagnosed with Alzheimer's dementia lack the corresponding neuropathology at autopsy, demonstrating the inaccuracy of purely symptom-based diagnosis (Section 4).
- 30% to 40% of cognitively unimpaired older adults possess Alzheimer's neuropathologic changes, highlighting a lengthy preclinical phase of the disease (Section 4).
- Individuals with both an amyloid biomarker and a second biomarker (tau or neurodegeneration) exhibit significantly faster cognitive decline than those with only one or neither (Section 8).

## Methods in brief

Consensus research framework and guideline update by the NIA-AA committee. The authors synthesized accumulated biomarker data and autopsy studies to establish the AT(N) biological classification system, creating a standardized language for observational and interventional research across the Alzheimer's continuum.

## Trial evidence

The AT(N) biological definition transformed clinical trial design. Modern anti-amyloid Phase 3 trials like [Clarity AD](/trials/lecanemab-clarity-ad-nct03887455) (lecanemab) and [TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2](/trials/donanemab-trailblazer-alz-2-nct04437511) (donanemab) utilized this framework to selectively enroll patients with confirmed pathology, ultimately yielding successful readouts. Conversely, preclinical trials enrolling solely based on biomarkers without cognitive symptoms, such as the [A4 Study](/research/papers/solanezumab-a4-prevention), failed to show cognitive benefit. While the framework authors explicitly cautioned that biological diagnosis was premature for routine clinical practice in 2018, recent FDA approvals for lecanemab and donanemab—which mandate amyloid confirmation—have effectively cemented the biological framework into mainstream care.

## Public sentiment

The AT(N) framework is polarizing. The scientific and biotech communities enthusiastically embrace it as a precision tool for clinical trials and regulatory endpoints. Conversely, clinical practitioners express skepticism about applying it outside research, citing the high cost of biomarker testing and the risk of over-diagnosing cognitively normal populations. Patients and caregivers often report anxiety regarding "asymptomatic Alzheimer's," frequently conflating a positive biomarker test with the inevitable rapid onset of severe dementia.

## Caveats

- The framework relies heavily on expensive and invasive testing like PET scans and lumbar punctures, creating a risk that diverse, lower-resource, or rural populations will be systematically excluded from cutting-edge research.
- A negative biomarker test only confirms that pathology falls below a specific in vivo detection threshold; it does not guarantee a complete absence of amyloid or tau in the brain.
- Neurodegeneration biomarkers (such as MRI atrophy) are completely nonspecific, making it difficult to untangle Alzheimer's-driven cognitive decline from other common comorbidities in older adults.
