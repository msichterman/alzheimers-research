## Takeaway

Blood-based biomarkers, particularly plasma p-tau217, demonstrate exceptional diagnostic accuracy and robustness for Alzheimer's disease. They are poised to transform clinical trial recruitment by drastically reducing the need for PET and CSF screening, though prospective validation in primary care is required before widespread clinical implementation.

## Key findings

- Plasma p-tau217 levels increase by 300–700% in symptomatic Alzheimer's, compared to a modest 8–15% drop in the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, providing superior diagnostic robustness (paper, BBMs as diagnostic biomarkers).
- Diagnostic algorithms using plasma p-tau217 can correctly classify approximately 80% of patients with mild cognitive impairment without requiring confirmatory CSF or PET testing (paper, BBMs as diagnostic biomarkers).
- Prescreening with high-performing blood biomarkers in preclinical trials could drastically reduce the number of PET scans required, as seen retrospectively in the A4 study which needed over 4,000 scans to find 1,169 eligible participants (paper, BBMs as prescreening biomarkers).

## Trial evidence

This review synthesizes observational and retrospective trial data rather than reporting new prospective results. It highlights how blood tests could optimize screening in large prevention trials (such as the [A4 study](/research/papers/solanezumab-a4-prevention)). However, the authors' cost-reduction models rely on retrospective screening data from failed therapeutic trials, rather than prospective interventional biomarker trials.

## Public sentiment

Public and professional sentiment is highly optimistic but somewhat divided by practical realities. Clinical neurologists are enthusiastically adopting p-tau217 to streamline workflows and replace invasive tests, while media hype about predicting the disease decades in advance has driven premature demand for asymptomatic screening, leading to patient confusion.

## Caveats

- Real-world performance in primary care remains unproven, as current accuracy claims rely heavily on retrospective analyses of specialized memory clinic cohorts.
- The authors' projected 2-4 year timeline for a clinical 'revolution' may be overly optimistic given the unresolved need for global assay standardization.
- While models suggest blood tests will drastically cut clinical trial costs, these savings are currently extrapolated from the screening phases of past therapeutic trials rather than proven in prospective diagnostic studies.

### Cross-examination

3 facts verified against the source document and trial registries. Flagged items:

- **Registry cross-check** — The document cites the A4 trial to model how blood tests reduce confirmatory PET/CSF prescreening requirements. However, the registry record (NCT02008357) shows A4 was a therapeutic trial for an anti-amyloid drug (solanezumab) that failed its primary cognitive endpoint, not a prospective trial evaluating biomarkers. The document relies entirely on retrospective analysis of A4's screening cohort to validate the biomarker utility.
- **Misinformation watch** — The assumption that a positive p-tau217 test in an asymptomatic, unimpaired person is a definitive guarantee they will rapidly develop clinical Alzheimer's dementia.
- **Misinformation watch** — Promoting the blood test results as a prompt for immediate, unproven alternative therapies (e.g., high-dose lithium supplements or specialized diets) to 'cure' or reverse the tau levels without clinical oversight.
