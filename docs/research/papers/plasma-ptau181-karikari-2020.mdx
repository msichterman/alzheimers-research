## Takeaway

An ultrasensitive blood test for p-tau181 accurately differentiates Alzheimer's disease from other neurodegenerative disorders and strongly predicts brain amyloid and tau pathology. This evidence established p-tau181 as a highly scalable, first-line clinical screening tool.

## Key findings

- Plasma p-tau181 levels increased progressively across the Alzheimer's continuum, from amyloid-negative young adults to those with Alzheimer's dementia (paper, Figure 1).
- The assay distinguished Alzheimer's disease from other neurodegenerative disorders, including frontotemporal dementia, with high accuracy (AUC 82.76% to 100%) (paper, Findings).
- Blood p-tau181 strongly predicted in vivo tau and amyloid-beta PET positivity, outperforming age and APOE ε4 status (paper, Figure 3).
- Baseline p-tau181 concentrations correlated with one-year cognitive decline and hippocampal atrophy (paper, Figure 4).

## Methods in brief

Prospective cohort study analyzing 1,131 participants across four clinic-based cohorts (discovery, two validation, and primary-care). Included cognitively unimpaired adults, individuals with mild cognitive impairment, Alzheimer's dementia, and other neurodegenerative diseases. Cross-sectional analysis with a limited one-year longitudinal follow-up (n=88).

## Trial evidence

This document establishes foundational data from the [BioFINDER-1](/trials/biofinder-1-nct01208675) and [BioFINDER-2](/trials/biofinder-2-nct03174938) cohorts, alongside the TRIAD cohort. While the one-year predictive claims rely on a small patient subset, subsequent independent cohorts (ADNI and DIAN) have replicated the findings, confirming plasma p-tau181 as a robust diagnostic and prescreening biomarker.

## Public sentiment

Public sentiment views the p-tau181 blood test as a breakthrough that democratizes Alzheimer's diagnostics by removing the bottleneck of expensive PET scans. However, patient communities report mixed feelings, expressing anxiety over receiving early diagnostic information without accessible cures and confusion about direct-to-consumer test marketing.

## Caveats

- The strong claims regarding one-year clinical and physical decline are heavily extrapolated from an extremely small subset of the total study population.
- Because different brain imaging techniques (PET ligands) were used in different study locations, some variation in the data may be driven by the clinical centers themselves rather than distinct biology.

### Cross-examination

4 facts verified against the source document and trial registries. Flagged items:

- **Registry cross-check** — The document presents data from a 'primary-care cohort' claiming it differentiates AD, but the formal Swedish BioFINDER Primary Care Study (NCT06120361) only officially began in January 2020 (and was posted to CT.gov in 2023). This indicates the paper likely used an early pilot or retrospective samples, and as noted in the paper's limitations, this specific cohort lacked independent PET or CSF biomarker confirmation for AD diagnosis.
- **Registry cross-check** — The document's claims about the biomarker predicting one-year cognitive decline and hippocampal atrophy are based on a heavily restricted longitudinal subset (n=88) rather than the full cohort of 1,131 participants, representing an extremely early interim analysis of long-term cohorts like BioFINDER-2 that are scheduled to run until 2036.
- **Misinformation watch** — The blood test provides a 100% definitive diagnosis of clinical Alzheimer's disease on its own.
- **Misinformation watch** — A positive p-tau181 test means a patient will inevitably and rapidly develop dementia.
