## Critical review
**Primary endpoint: not_met.** Neither coprimary outcome was met at week 78: ADAS-cog worsening was numerically greater with verubecestat than with placebo (7.9 and 8.0 vs. 7.7; P=0.63 and P=0.46), a direction favouring placebo, and the ADCS-ADL differences were not significant (P=0.49 and P=0.32); the trial was stopped early for futility.
> The declines in the verubecestat groups did not differ significantly from those in the placebo group at week 78 for either of the coprimary outcomes.
**Multiplicity handling:** Bonferroni correction applied to both verubecestat dose levels, in conjunction with a closed sequential testing approach controlling the type 1 error rate, testing primary outcomes and then secondary outcomes in the order specified in the statistical plan

### Risk of bias

- **sponsor analysis** — Data were collected by the investigators but analyzed by the sponsor (Merck), which also designed the trial and funded it.
- **sponsor-employed medical writer** — The first manuscript draft was written by a medical writer employed by the sponsor together with the first author.
- **attrition** — Roughly 30% of randomized patients did not complete the 78-week part 1, and premature withdrawal for adverse events was more frequent in both verubecestat groups than in placebo.
- **early termination for futility** — The trial was stopped by the data and safety monitoring committee for futility in February 2017, 5 months before the scheduled completion of part 1, and no patient completed part 2.
- **missing-data handling** — Planned not-missing-at-random sensitivity analyses were abandoned because the trial was negative, so the robustness of the efficacy analyses to missing data was not examined.
- **diagnostic misclassification at entry** — No amyloid biomarker was required at screening, so patients with non-amyloid dementia could enter; the authors argue substudy data make this unlikely to explain the result.
- **ARIA ascertainment** — Routine MRI monitoring for amyloid-related imaging abnormalities was discontinued partway through the trial, so ARIA ascertainment was incomplete and no ARIA counts are reported.
- **selection into biomarker substudies** — Patients were not randomly selected into the PET amyloid and cerebrospinal fluid substudies; participation depended on site willingness and proximity to a PET ligand facility.
- **post-randomization exclusions from the analysed population** — The first 200 patients enrolled and the 53 patients assigned to the 60-mg dose were excluded from the primary efficacy and safety analyses.

### Limitations stated by the document

- A limitation of our trial was the lack of requirement for the presence of an amyloid biomarker at screening, thereby potentially allowing patients who had dementia owing to nonamyloid-related disease to be included.
- Bias may have been introduced in the uncontrolled selection of patients for the biomarker substudies, but the demographic characteristics of these patients were similar to those of the overall population of the trial.
- Although subgroup analyses that were based on patient characteristics were prespecified, the trial was not adequately powered for such analyses.
- Sensitivity analyses that were planned to explore the results of the efficacy analyses under the assumption that data were not missing at random were not performed given the negative results of the trial.
- The trial was terminated early for futility 50 months after onset, which was within 5 months before its scheduled completion, and after enrollment of the planned 1958 patients was complete.
- Similar reductions have been reported with other antiamyloid therapies, and the clinical significance of this finding is unknown.

### Where authoritative sources disagree

*Stored as structured evidence with each position attributed to its source, not resolved into a single verdict.*

- **Does the amyloid hypothesis hold once Alzheimer's dementia is clinically established?** — This trial concludes that near-maximal BACE-1 inhibition for 78 weeks did not slow clinical progression, which suggests either that progression is independent of A-beta production once dementia is present or that the amyloid hypothesis may not be correct. *(the authors of this trial (Egan et al., New England Journal of Medicine))*
- **Do antiamyloid drugs necessarily cause amyloid-related imaging abnormalities?** — Verubecestat, a BACE-1 inhibitor, was not found to be associated with the ARIA seen with some other antiamyloid therapies. *(the authors of this trial (Egan et al., New England Journal of Medicine))*
- **Could an inadequate dose explain the negative result?** — No — CSF A-beta was reduced by 63 to 81%, confirming target engagement, and phase 1 modeling indicated a 60-mg dose would not be substantially more effective than 40 mg. *(the authors of this trial (Egan et al., New England Journal of Medicine))*
- **Could enrollment of patients without amyloid pathology explain the negative result?** — No — approximately 90% of substudy patients had findings consistent with Alzheimer's disease and about 63% were APOE4 carriers, so the non-amyloid proportion was probably small. *(the authors of this trial (Egan et al., New England Journal of Medicine))*
- **What does the greater hippocampal volume loss under verubecestat mean?** — The document reports greater hippocampal volume reduction with verubecestat than placebo, notes similar reductions with other antiamyloid therapies, and states the clinical significance is unknown rather than adjudicating it. *(the authors of this trial (Egan et al., New England Journal of Medicine))*
- **When should amyloid-targeting treatment be given?** — The document endorses the proposal that amyloid-targeting treatment should be given early, before the onset of clinical symptoms. *(the authors of this trial (Egan et al., New England Journal of Medicine), citing a prior proposal)*
