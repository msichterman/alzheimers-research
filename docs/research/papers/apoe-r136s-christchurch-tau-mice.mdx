**Source**: [Nature Neuroscience, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-023-01480-8) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37957317)
**Authors**: Nelson et al. — Gladstone Institutes / UCSF

## Takeaway

The rare APOE-R136S (Christchurch) mutation completely protects against APOE4-driven tau pathology, neurodegeneration, and neuroinflammation in preclinical models when homozygous, but only offers partial structural protection when heterozygous. The mutation appears to work by disrupting APOE's binding to heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs), which subsequently blunts neuronal tau uptake.

## Key findings

- Homozygous APOE-R136S mutation fully rescued hippocampal volume and significantly reduced tau accumulation and gliosis in PS19-E4 tauopathy mice (paper, Figures 1, 4, 5).
- Heterozygous mutation provided partial protection against neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation, but did not significantly reduce tau pathology (paper, Figures 1, 4).
- In human iPSC-derived neurons, the homozygous mutation reduced tau uptake by roughly 50% by impairing the HSPG pathway (paper, Figure 3).
- Single-nucleus RNA sequencing showed the mutation dose-dependently increased disease-protective homeostatic glial populations and virtually eliminated disease-associated astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes (paper, Figures 6-8).

## Methods in brief

In vivo and in vitro study evaluating human APOE4 knock-in tauopathy mice (PS19-E4) and CRISPR-edited human iPSC-derived neurons. Mice (n=20-31 per group) were evaluated at 6 and 10 months of age. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing evaluated 175,080 nuclei from 15 mice.

## Trial evidence

There are currently no clinical trials evaluating the APOE-R136S mutation or R136S-mimicking therapies in humans; the evidence remains strictly preclinical. However, the broader strategy of introducing protective APOE variants to APOE4 carriers is being tested clinically, notably in the [LX1001-01](/trials/lx1001-01-nct03634007) trial, which uses gene therapy to deliver the protective APOE2 allele.

## Public sentiment

Public perception is highly optimistic, anchored to the dramatic narrative of the original Colombian patient who delayed early-onset Alzheimer's by decades. However, online longevity and biohacker communities frequently conflate the profound protection of the rare homozygous mutation with the much milder effects of the heterozygous mutation, and sometimes mistakenly assume that interventions mimicking this genetic effect are already available.

## Caveats

- The preclinical models isolate tau pathology without amyloid-beta plaques, meaning the mutation's ability to protect against amyloid-driven inflammatory and neurodegenerative effects remains unknown.
- The study engineered the mutation onto the high-risk APOE4 allele to test rescue effects, whereas the original highly protected human case occurred on the neutral APOE3 allele.
- The finding that a single copy (heterozygous) fails to prevent tau pathology sharply contrasts with public expectations of broad protection.
