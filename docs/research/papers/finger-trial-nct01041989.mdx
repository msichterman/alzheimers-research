**Authors**: Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare

## Takeaway

The FINGER trial provides landmark proof-of-concept that dementia risk is modifiable through lifestyle changes. It demonstrated that a structured, multi-domain approach—combining diet, exercise, cognitive training, and vascular monitoring—can significantly slow cognitive decline in high-risk older adults.

## Key findings

- A 2-year multidomain intervention significantly improved or maintained cognitive functioning compared to standard counseling ([The Lancet, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25771249/)).
- Secondary analyses suggest the intervention reduces the estimated overall relative risk of dementia ([JAD, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34151805/)).
- The WW-FINGERS network expanded these findings; the US POINTER trial confirmed that structured lifestyle interventions offer greater cognitive benefit than self-guided approaches ([JAMA, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40720610/)).

## Methods in brief

An interventional, randomized, single-masked prevention trial of 1,200 adults aged 60-77 with an increased dementia risk score and mild cognitive changes. The primary intervention spanned 2 years, with an ongoing extended follow-up phase tracking long-term dementia incidence. Registered as NCT01041989.

## Trial evidence

While the official ClinicalTrials.gov record remains outdated with no results posted, the FINGER trial successfully completed its primary phase in 2013. Its positive results were published in The Lancet in 2015, establishing it as the gold standard for non-pharmacological prevention. The protocol directly catalyzed the World-Wide FINGERS (WW-FINGERS) network, notably driving the pivotal [US POINTER](/trials/us-pointer-nct03688126) replication.

## Public sentiment

Public perception is highly hopeful, especially within high-risk communities (like APOE4 carriers) and longevity circles who value the intervention as an empowering, actionable strategy in late life. Scientists praise the trial for shifting the paradigm toward proactive risk reduction. However, educated advocates must frequently temper lay media coverage that mistakenly frames the modest risk-reduction effect as a 'cure' for existing Alzheimer's or conflates it with biomarker blood tests.

## Caveats

- The intervention bends the slope of cognitive decline; it is a preventative measure for at-risk individuals, not a treatment or cure for existing clinical dementia.
- Implementing the multi-domain protocol requires significant patient adherence and structural support, which may challenge real-world scalability.
- Definitive proof that the intervention delays the actual clinical onset of dementia requires final data from the extended follow-up, expected to complete in late 2025.
