## Takeaway

Targeting a systolic blood pressure of &lt;120 mm Hg does not significantly reduce the risk of probable dementia compared to a standard &lt;140 mm Hg target. However, it does significantly reduce the risk of developing mild cognitive impairment (MCI). The trial was stopped early due to cardiovascular benefits, which likely left the long-term dementia endpoint statistically underpowered.

## Key findings

- Probable dementia incidence was 7.2 vs 8.6 cases per 1000 person-years for intensive vs standard control, failing to reach statistical significance (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.67-1.04) (paper, Results).
- Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) risk was significantly reduced, with 14.6 vs 18.3 cases per 1000 person-years (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.69-0.95) (paper, Results).
- The composite outcome of MCI or probable dementia was significantly reduced (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.74-0.97) (paper, Results).

## Methods in brief

Randomized clinical trial involving 9,361 adults aged 50 years or older with hypertension but without diabetes or history of stroke. The median intervention period was 3.34 years, with a total median follow-up of 5.11 years ([NCT01206062](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01206062)).

## Trial evidence

This paper reports the cognitive outcomes of the NIH-sponsored [SPRINT MIND trial](/trials/sprint-mind-nct01206062). The overarching trial was terminated early due to highly significant cardiovascular and mortality benefits in the intensive treatment group. This shortened follow-up resulted in fewer incident cases of dementia than initially projected, likely underpowering the study for its specific cognitive endpoint. While probable dementia was the primary focus of this sub-study, it was listed as a secondary outcome for the main SPRINT trial.

## Public sentiment

Public and clinical reception is sharply divided. Patients, caregivers, and longevity communities enthusiastically view the trial as an actionable lifestyle intervention for cognitive preservation, focusing heavily on the MCI reduction. Conversely, practicing physicians express widespread skepticism about real-world implementation. Clinicians caution that aggressive blood pressure targets (&lt;120 mm Hg) in the elderly can lead to dangerous side effects like falls and hypotension.

## Caveats

- The trial's early termination at 3.3 years severely limited the statistical power needed to detect differences in long-term dementia incidence.
- Aggressive blood pressure control in elderly populations carries significant practical risks, including falls and hypoperfusion, which may complicate clinical application for frail patients.
