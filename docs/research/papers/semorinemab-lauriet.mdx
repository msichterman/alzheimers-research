## Takeaway

In the Phase 2 Lauriet trial, the anti-tau antibody semorinemab significantly slowed cognitive decline on the ADAS-Cog11. However, the trial failed its functional coprimary endpoint and all secondary clinical and biomarker measures, leading experts to view the isolated cognitive signal as a false positive.

## Key findings

- Reduced cognitive decline by 42.2% on the ADAS-Cog11 at week 49 compared to placebo (p = 0.0008) (paper, Table 2).
- Failed to show a significant treatment effect on the functional ADCS-ADL scale or on global clinical measures like CDR-SB and MMSE (paper, Table 2).
- Did not alter the rate of tau accumulation on PET imaging or cerebral atrophy on MRI (paper, Table 2).
- Elevated plasma tau biomarkers >20-fold, demonstrating peripheral target engagement without downstream disease modification (paper, Results).

## Methods in brief

Multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2 trial of 272 adults aged 50–85 with probable mild-to-moderate AD. Participants received 4,500 mg IV semorinemab or placebo every four weeks for 48 or 60 weeks. Registered as [NCT03828747](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03828747).

## Trial evidence

This paper reports results from the Phase 2 [Lauriet](/trials/semorinemab-lauriet-nct03828747) trial. The broader clinical evidence for semorinemab as a disease-modifying therapy is very weak. Despite Lauriet's isolated cognitive hit, a larger Phase 2 trial in earlier-stage AD ([Tauriel](/research/papers/semorinemab-tauriel)) unambiguously failed its primary endpoint, leading to the program's apparent discontinuation.

## Public sentiment

Public perception briefly diverged from the scientific evidence when early corporate press releases highlighted the isolated cognitive signal, sparking a retail stock rally for AC Immune. However, the scientific and medical communities remained highly skeptical, recognizing that cognitive benefits without functional or biomarker improvements likely represented a statistical artifact.

## Caveats

- The study cohort was predominantly White (89%) and lacked racial and ethnic diversity, limiting the generalizability of the findings.
- The COVID-19 pandemic caused missed doses and altered assessment protocols, which could introduce unaccounted variance despite preplanned sensitivity analyses.
- A post-hoc analysis revealed the cognitive benefit was driven primarily by a single 'word recognition' item, undermining confidence in the drug's true clinical efficacy.

### Cross-examination

2 facts verified against the source document and trial registries. Flagged items:

- **Fact check (corrected)** — The Lauriet trial cohort was strictly 'moderate' severity, meaning the paper's 'mild-to-moderate' label is a discrepancy. The clinical-trials researcher incorrectly flagged this. Paper Table 1 shows 90% of the cohort had a CDR-Global Score of 1 (mild AD) and only 10% had a 2 (moderate). The population is accurately described as mild-to-moderate.
- **Registry cross-check** — The document repeatedly describes the Lauriet trial population as having 'mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease', but the ClinicalTrials.gov registry strictly defines the cohort as having 'moderate severity' based on an MMSE inclusion criterion of 16-21.
- **Misinformation watch** — Claims that semorinemab is an effective disease-modifying treatment based solely on the isolated ADAS-Cog11 result, ignoring its failure to prevent functional decline or alter tau PET accumulation.
- **Misinformation watch** — Lexical confusion on social media blending 'semorinemab' with 'sermorelin', a completely unrelated growth hormone-releasing peptide.
